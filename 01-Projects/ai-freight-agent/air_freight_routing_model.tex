\begin{abstract}
Formal specification of the optimization model for the air freight multi-commodity routing
problem. The model is a Binary Multi-Commodity Flow problem on a commodity-specific subgraph
of the air freight network (see \texttt{docs/air\_freight\_multi\_shipment\_graph.drawio}
and \texttt{docs/air\_freight\_shipment\_subgraph.drawio}). Scope: single-shipment routing
across multiple flight legs (direct and multi-hop) with two air capacity layers — contracted
ULD allocation (Block Space Agreements) and spot rate-card pricing. Probabilistic transit
time objectives, through-ULD as a decision variable, period-level utilization commitments,
and full bin-packing inside ULDs are deferred. The model parallels the Ocean FCL formulation
(\texttt{model/ocean\_fcl\_routing.tex}) for notational consistency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Statement}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

Given a set of air shipment requests (commodities), each defined by origin location, destination
location, cargo volume, chargeable weight, and time constraints, find a minimum-cost assignment
of commodities to paths through the multimodal air freight network such that:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item every commodity is delivered on or before its deadline,
  \item no flight leg exceeds its ULD physical capacity (volume and weight per ULD),
  \item no Block Space Agreement (BSA) exceeds its contracted per-flight ULD allocation,
  \item period-level allocation caps are respected,
  \item cargo cutoff times are honored,
  \item hub minimum connect times are honored, including the through-ULD vs.\ re-ULD distinction, and
  \item optional per-commodity budget caps are respected.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{MVP scope.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Single-shipment routing; each shipment routes independently subject to shared supply.
  \item Each scheduled flight leg is one arc. Multi-stop flights (same flight number, intermediate
        stops) are decomposed into multiple arcs for transit time visibility.
  \item ULD types: LD3, LD7, PMC pallet, AKE. Optimizer selects ULD type per shipment per flight.
  \item Two air supply layers per flight: (a) contracted ULD allocation under one or more BSAs;
        (b) spot rate-card at IATA weight breaks.
  \item Pivot weight (take-or-pay minimum) modeled on hard BSA contracts.
  \item Through-ULD vs.\ re-ULD at hub modeled via pre-computed compatibility parameter
        $\psi$, with rehandling cost and MCT differentiated. Explicit decision variable for
        through-ULD selection deferred to P1.
  \item Period-level minimum utilization commitments (hard BSA take-or-pay across period)
        are computed by a separate upstream model and surfaced as fixed per-run targets.
        This MILP does not solve them jointly.
  \item Objective: minimize total air + ground freight cost subject to deterministic
        time-window feasibility. Probabilistic on-time delivery objective deferred to P1.
  \item Hazmat / perishable / valuable cargo type segregation: model parameter, not a hard MVP
        constraint (recorded for future restriction logic).
  \item \textbf{Scope boundary --- sell rate and margin are upstream of this MILP.}
        Sell rate to the shipper is set by a separate quote engine at booking time
        (cost-plus-margin, market-rate comparison, competitor signals); once a shipment
        is booked, the price is fixed and the routing problem is pure cost
        minimization. The MILP outputs cost; the quote engine composes
        $\text{margin} = \text{sell} - \text{cost}$ for operator-facing screens. Sell
        rate is stored on the shipment record (\texttt{shipment\_attributes.md} \S2.5)
        but is not an input to the routing optimization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Time-zone Convention}
\label{sec:tz-convention}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

All timestamps, time-window parameters, decision-variable arrival times $t_k(n)$, and
constraint-side time bounds in this model are expressed in \textbf{Coordinated Universal
Time (UTC)}. This convention is normative for every internal computation; conversions to
local time happen only at I/O boundaries (operator display, customer notifications,
external API payloads keyed on origin-local or destination-local).

\paragraph{Why UTC internally.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item IATA SSIM Chapter~6 (Airport Coordination Procedures) standardizes slot
        coordination messaging on UTC — the regulatory channel between airlines and slot
        coordinators uses UTC for all dates, days, and times.\footnote{IATA Standard
        Schedules Information Manual, Chapter 6 --- Airport Coordination Procedures.
        \url{https://www.iata.org/en/store/publications/manuals-standards-and-regulations/standard-schedule-information-manual-chapter-6airport-coordination-procedures__ssim-ch6/}}
  \item Flight planning and Air Traffic Control universally use UTC (``Zulu time'').
  \item Date-line crossings (e.g., TPE$\to$SFO) and daylight-saving transitions create
        ambiguity in local time and disappear as categories in UTC arithmetic.
  \item Cross-leg arithmetic ($\text{ETA}_f + \text{MCT} \leq \text{ETD}_g$) is direct in
        UTC; in local time it would require hop-by-hop conversion at every constraint.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Ingestion of SSIM-style sources.}
SSIM messages carry a Time Mode field with value \texttt{U} (UTC) or \texttt{L} (local);
the Local mode records include a paired UTC offset field
(\texttt{utc\_local\_time\_variation\_departure} and the arrival analog).\footnote{Octallium,
``Flight Schedules From SSIM'' (technical analysis, 2025).
\url{https://www.octallium.com/blog/2025/02/27/flight-schedules-from-ssim/}} At
ingestion, any record in Time Mode \texttt{L} is converted to UTC using the embedded
offset; only the resulting UTC timestamps enter the model. Where source data omits an
offset, ingestion fails fast with a structured error --- the model does not silently
infer a timezone.

\paragraph{Cutoffs and customs filing deadlines.}
$\text{CGC}_{f,\tau}$, $\text{DCO}_f$, $\text{AMS}_f$, $\text{ICS2}_f$, $\text{ACI}_f$ (\S\ref{sec:flight-params}),
the shipment-indexed effective cutoff $\text{CO}_{f,k}^*$, and prep-time-adjusted
feasibility bounds are all UTC absolute timestamps. The carrier-side cutoff is published
in local time at the origin airport; ingestion converts at intake.

\paragraph{Daylight Saving Time.}
IATA publishes seasonal schedules (Summer + Winter); UTC offsets in flight records change
with the season. Working in UTC absorbs DST entirely --- no DST math anywhere in the
model. Schedules near a DST transition simply carry the post-transition UTC offset in the
SSIM record, and ingestion produces correct UTC timestamps either way.

\paragraph{Date-line crossings.}
A flight whose ETD and ETA fall on the same UTC date but different local dates is
unambiguous in UTC. The SSIM format handles this via the operational suffix `Z' for
flights using the same flight number on the same UTC date, and via the Overnight
Indicator attached to Departure time for overmidnight aircraft layovers. The MILP sees
only the UTC timestamps; the date-line is invisible to constraint evaluation.

\paragraph{User-facing display.}
Operator dashboards and customer-facing screens convert UTC back to local time at the
relevant station (origin for cargo cutoff and pickup; destination for ETA, deadline, and
delivery). The conversion is a presentation-layer concern, not a model concern.

\section{Commercial Structure: MAWB and HAWB}
\label{sec:mawb-hawb}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

Air freight is contracted, billed, and routed at two distinct document layers — Master
Air Waybill (MAWB) and House Air Waybill (HAWB). The economic mechanism mid-market
forwarders depend on (consolidation of many small HAWBs into one MAWB to access lower
tier-break rates) is invisible if a model treats every shipment as an independent
tender. This section defines the commercial structure that the formulation must
represent.

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2cm, labelwidth=1.8cm, style=nextline]
  \item[MAWB] \emph{Master Air Waybill.} The contract of carriage between the airline
        and the freight forwarder. Airline bills the forwarder against one MAWB per
        shipment from origin airport to destination airport. 11-digit number, 3-digit
        IATA airline prefix (e.g., \texttt{160-12345678} for Cathay).
        The airline does not see what is inside the MAWB at the pricing level.
  \item[HAWB] \emph{House Air Waybill.} The contract of carriage between the freight
        forwarder and the underlying shipper. One HAWB per (shipper, consignee) pair.
        Issued under the forwarder's own numbering scheme.
        Airline does not see HAWBs in pricing, but customs at destination does
        (AMS / ICS2 / equivalent filings are per-HAWB).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Filing mechanics.}
The MAWB is filed electronically with the airline via IATA Cargo-IMP messages
(\texttt{FWB} = waybill data, \texttt{FHL} = list of HAWBs underneath) or Cargo-XML /
ONE Record. The cargo manifest (CARGOMAN) lists all HAWBs under a given MAWB and
travels with the physical cargo. At destination, customs receives both the MAWB
(carriage record) and each HAWB (as the underlying consignment requiring clearance).

\subsection{Universality}

The MAWB / HAWB structure applies to \emph{every} air freight shipment regardless of
how the forwarder procured capacity. The procurement mode (contracted BSA, spot rate,
co-loader / GSA wholesale buy, dynamic NAC quote) determines how the MAWB is
\emph{rated}, not whether the MAWB exists. Capacity-side modeling and document-side
modeling are orthogonal.

\subsection{Direct MAWB (Straight MAWB)}

Single large shipment from one shipper to one consignee may be tendered without a
separate HAWB: the MAWB serves as both the airline contract and the shipper contract.
This is a degenerate case where one MAWB carries exactly one HAWB-equivalent
consignment. Common for shipments above \(\sim 1{,}000\,\text{kg}\) or for high-value
single-piece moves. The MVP formulation must support this as a special case (one
HAWB per MAWB), not as a separate construct.

\subsection{Co-loader (Neutral Consolidator) Pattern}

A forwarder without direct carrier allocation may tender cargo as a HAWB under
another forwarder's MAWB (the co-loader holds the carrier paper). From the small
forwarder's perspective, the co-loader is a procurement option priced at all-in
\$/kg with no visibility into the underlying MAWB structure. From the co-loader's
perspective, the small forwarder's HAWB is just one of many HAWBs being consolidated.
Co-loader procurement is treated in the supply layer as one of the rate-function
classes (see Section~\ref{sec:bsa-params}, P1 generalization), not as a separate
document model.

\subsection{Why Consolidation Matters Economically}

Airline rate cards apply tier breaks (IATA standard: 45, 100, 300, 500, 1000 kg, plus
custom higher breaks on negotiated contracts) at the \emph{MAWB} chargeable weight,
not per HAWB. The higher the MAWB weight, the lower the marginal \$/kg.

\paragraph{Worked example (TPE\(\to\)JFK, eight 150 kg shipments).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Direct tender (each shipment as its own Direct MAWB at \(+100\) tier
        \(\sim \$4.80\)/kg): airline cost \(8 \times 150 \times \$4.80 = \$5{,}760\).
  \item Consol into one MAWB at 1{,}200 kg total weight at \(+1000\) tier
        \(\sim \$2.20\)/kg: airline cost \(1{,}200 \times \$2.20 = \$2{,}640\).
\end{itemize}
At a forwarder retail HAWB rate of \(\$4.50\)/kg (collecting \(\$5{,}400\) revenue),
direct tender yields a \$360 loss while consol yields a \$2{,}760 margin. The
optimization model must be able to find this consolidation; if it cannot, it
systematically over-recommends direct tendering and under-quotes the forwarder's
margin envelope.

\subsection{Constraints That Bind on Consolidation}

HAWBs may share one MAWB only if all of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,label=(\alph*)]
  \item Same origin airport \(o_{\text{AP}}(k)\) and same destination airport
        \(d_{\text{AP}}(k)\).
  \item Same routing through the air network — every HAWB on the MAWB takes the same
        flight sequence (i.e., the same set of \(A_{\text{AIR}}\) arcs).
  \item Cargo-type compatibility — DGR rules at the MAWB level (one DGR HAWB makes
        the entire MAWB a DGR shipment; carrier and ULD must accept DGR).
  \item Booking-window compatibility — all HAWBs must be ready by the MAWB's CGC.
  \item Aggregate weight / volume fits the ULD set claimed for the MAWB.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{MAWB Chargeable Weight: Density Mixing}
\label{sec:mawb-cw}

Airline billing applies the volumetric rule at the \emph{MAWB} level, not per HAWB.
For an MAWB \(m\) carrying HAWBs \(\{k : h_{k,m} = 1\}\):

\begin{equation}
  \text{CW}_m \defeq \max\!\left(
    (1 + \delta) \cdot \sum_{k} h_{k,m} \cdot w_k,\;
    \sum_{k} h_{k,m} \cdot v_k \cdot 167
  \right)
  \label{eq:mawb-cw}
\end{equation}

where \(\delta\) is the dunnage / strapping factor (typically 3--5\%, carrier-specific;
default 0.05).

\paragraph{Density mixing benefit.}
This MAWB-level computation can yield strictly less chargeable weight than summing
HAWB-level chargeable weights, because dense cargo absorbs the volumetric ``slack''
of light cargo. Worked example:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
HAWB & Actual wt & Volume & Volumetric (\(\times 167\)) & HAWB CW \\
\midrule
\(H_1\) (machinery) & 200 kg & 0.5 m\textsuperscript{3} & 84 kg & 200 kg \\
\(H_2\) (apparel)   & 60 kg  & 1.2 m\textsuperscript{3} & 200 kg & 200 kg \\
\midrule
\textbf{Naïve sum of HAWB CW} & & & & \textbf{400 kg} \\
\textbf{MAWB-level} (\(\delta=0\)) & 260 kg & 1.7 m\textsuperscript{3} & 284 kg & \textbf{284 kg} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The 116 kg saving is captured only by computing CW at the MAWB level. A model that
sums HAWB CWs and then applies the tier-break rate cannot see this — it must
aggregate physical quantities first.

\paragraph{Modeling note.}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mawb-cw} is a \(\max\) of two linear expressions in the binary
variables \(h_{k,m}\). It linearizes identically to the existing pivot-weight
formulation (Section~\ref{sec:linearization}, P.10 pattern): introduce
\(\text{CW}_m \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) with two lower-bound inequalities. The
linearization is exact because the objective minimizes the rate-tier function applied
to \(\text{CW}_m\), pushing it down to the binding right-hand side.

\subsection{Rate Function on MAWB Chargeable Weight}
\label{sec:rate-function}

The airline-billable cost of an MAWB on a flight is a function of the MAWB
chargeable weight. The MVP v1 formulation hard-codes two cases: (a) IATA tier-break
spot rate (Eq.~\ref{eq:spot-cost}) and (b) pivot-weight BSA. Real procurement spans
more shapes:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.3cm, labelwidth=2.1cm, style=nextline]
  \item[TACT] IATA tiers (\(-45, +45, +100, +300, +500, +1000\), plus negotiated
        \(+2000, +3000\)). Piecewise-linear with ``next-break-down'' minimum.
  \item[SCR] Specific Commodity Rate — discounted lane rate for a 4-digit IATA
        commodity code. Same piecewise structure, lower rates.
  \item[CCR] Class Rate — multiplier on general cargo (e.g., VAL \(+50\%\),
        DGR \(+25\%\), AVI \(+75\%\)).
  \item[BUC / Pivot] Flat charge per ULD up to a pivot weight, marginal rate above.
        Generalization of the current P.10 pivot model.
  \item[NAC / Block Rate] Flat \(\$/\)kg, no tier breaks; all surcharges embedded.
        Common from co-loaders, GSAs, negotiated lane contracts.
  \item[Dynamic Spot] Real-time per-flight quote with rate validity TTL
        (WebCargo, cargo.one, Awery). Flat \(\$/\)kg over the quoted weight band
        for the TTL window.
\end{description}

\paragraph{V2 generalization: piecewise-linear rate function.}
The v2 restructure replaces the two hard-coded rate shapes with a single
mechanism: each contract \(c\) carries a list of \((b_i, m_i)\) tuples — segment
breakpoints \(b_0 = 0 < b_1 < b_2 < \dots\) and marginal rates \(m_0, m_1, \dots\) —
that define the rate as a piecewise-linear function of MAWB chargeable weight:
\begin{equation}
  R_c(\text{CW}) \;=\; \text{floor}_c \;+\; \sum_i m_i \cdot
    \min\!\bigl(\max(\text{CW} - b_i, 0),\; b_{i+1} - b_i\bigr)
  \label{eq:rate-fn}
\end{equation}
TACT, SCR, BUC, NAC, BSA-pivot, dynamic spot are all special cases:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Rate type & Encoding as \((b_i, m_i)\) tuples \\
\midrule
TACT (TPE-JFK GEN) & \(\{(0, 6.00), (45, 5.50), (100, 4.80), (300, 4.20), (500, 3.80), (1000, 3.20)\}\) \\
  & \quad + IATA next-break-down rule (non-convex check) + min charge floor \\
BUC (one LD3) & \(\{(0, 0), (750, 0.80)\}\) + floor \$1{,}200 \\
NAC / Block & \(\{(0, 3.50)\}\) (single segment) \\
BSA-pivot tiered & \(\{(0, 0), (1000, 0.65), (2000, 0.50)\}\) + floor \$900 \\
Dynamic spot & \(\{(0, \text{quoted rate})\}\) + TTL validation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Worked comparison.}
Same lane, four procurement options, three MAWB chargeable weights:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\(\text{CW}_m\) & TACT (A) & BUC (B) & NAC (C) & BSA-pivot (D) \\
\midrule
200 kg & \$960 & \$1{,}200 & \$700 & \$900 \\
800 kg & \$3{,}040 & \$1{,}240 & \$2{,}800 & \$900 \\
1{,}500 kg & \$4{,}800 & \$1{,}800 & \$5{,}250 & \$1{,}225 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At 200 kg NAC wins; at 800 kg and 1500 kg BSA-pivot dominates. The optimizer
must choose per-contract, per-MAWB. A single hardcoded cost expression cannot
capture this — hence the per-contract piecewise-linear function.

\paragraph{MILP encoding via supply-option assignment.}
The cleanest formulation does \emph{not} use SOS-2 constraints on a continuous CW.
Instead, decompose each contract's rate function into a set of \emph{supply options},
where each option's cost is linear or convex within itself. The global non-convexity
of the rate function emerges from a \(\min\) over options, captured by a single
assignment binary per option:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    & y_{f,m,o} \in \{0, 1\}: \text{MAWB } m \text{ on flight } f \text{ uses supply option } o \\
    & \sum_{o \in O(c)} y_{f,m,o} = 1 \quad \text{(exactly one option per MAWB-flight)} \\
    & \text{cost}_{f,m} = \sum_{o} c_o(\text{CW}_m) \cdot y_{f,m,o}
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Each option \(o\) is, e.g., one IATA tier, one BSA pivot segment, one NAC block.
Within the option, \(c_o(\text{CW}_m)\) is linear (NAC, single TACT tier) or convex
(pivot floor + overage = flat then linear ramp). Convex within an option needs no
further binaries (LP relaxation is tight on convex PWL minimization).

\paragraph{Why this works: the convexity asymmetry.}
For minimization of a piecewise-linear function, LP relaxation is tight only when
the function is convex (slopes non-decreasing). Concave PWL (slopes non-increasing,
e.g., the volume-discount shape of air freight rates) causes the LP to interpolate
across the curve via the convex hull, underestimating the true cost. Worked example
on a concave PWL with marginal rates \((6.00, 5.50, 4.80)\) on \([0,45], [45,100],
[100, 300]\):

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item True \(f(100) = 6 \cdot 45 + 5.5 \cdot 55 = 572.50\).
  \item LP with continuous \(\lambda\)-formulation, no SOS-2: \(\lambda_0 = 2/3\),
        \(\lambda_3 = 1/3\) yields \(\text{CW} = 100\), cost \(= 1532.5/3 = 510.83\).
        Underestimate of \$61.67 ($\sim 11\%$).
\end{itemize}

Decomposing into supply options avoids this trap entirely: each option is convex
within itself, and the assignment binary \(y_{f,m,o}\) selects which option's
cost applies. The optimizer naturally picks the cheapest option, which reconstructs
the \(\min\) envelope — equivalent to enforcing SOS-2 on a continuous PWL but
operationally cleaner.

\paragraph{Binary count.}
For TPE-JFK with 6 IATA tiers + BSA-A (2-piece pivot, 2 options) + BSA-B (3-piece
tiered, 3 options) + 1 NAC: 12 supply-option binaries per MAWB-flight pair, with
one exactly-one constraint. Linear scaling in number of supply options per lane.
At MVP scale (\(|K| \sim 100\), \(|F^k| \sim 30\), \(\sim 10\) options per flight):
\(\sim 30{,}000\) supply-option binaries — tractable for HiGHS.

This unifies the procurement layer and removes the practitioner-flagged
2015-vs-2026 disconnect. Implementation deferred to v2.

\subsection{Volume Measurement}

Volume is computed from piece dimensions:
\begin{equation*}
  v_k = \sum_{p \in \text{pieces}(k)} \frac{L_p \cdot W_p \cdot H_p}{10^6} \;\;[\text{m}^3]
\end{equation*}
with dimensions in cm and bounding-box convention (smallest enclosing cuboid for
irregular pieces). Non-stackable cargo (NSTK flag) incurs a \(1.2\)--\(1.5\times\)
multiplier on \(v_k\), applied at subgraph construction.

\subsection{Weight- vs.\ Volume-Binding ULDs}

For typical mid-market mixed cargo (\(\sim 60\%\) above the 167 kg/m\textsuperscript{3}
breakeven), the binding constraint on a ULD is determined by ULD geometry, not just
cargo:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccl}
\toprule
ULD type & Volume & Weight cap & Volumetric @ 167 & Typically binds on... \\
\midrule
LD3 / AKE & 4.5 m\textsuperscript{3} & 1{,}587 kg & 752 kg & Weight \\
LD7       & 11.1 m\textsuperscript{3} & 4{,}626 kg & 1{,}854 kg & Weight \\
PMC pallet & 7.5 m\textsuperscript{3} & 6{,}804 kg & 1{,}253 kg & Volume \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

PMC pallets are weight-over-built relative to volume — cargo cubes out long before
weight cap. LD3 / LD7 are balanced toward weight-binding for typical cargo density.
This affects ULD selection in the optimization: light/bulky shipments prefer PMC;
dense cargo prefers LD3/LD7 to maximize weight per ULD position. Both P.2 (volume)
and P.3 (weight) constraints must be retained — the binding one switches by cargo
mix.

\subsection{Modeling Implication (Formal Restructure in Section~\ref{sec:deferred})}

The MVP v1 formulation in this document treats each shipment as a single tender
(implicit Direct MAWB per shipment). This degrades the model's representational
fidelity for any forwarder that consolidates. The v2 restructure (to be drafted in
the next revision) re-roots the decision variables around MAWBs:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \(M\) — set of candidate MAWBs (decision: which MAWBs are created).
  \item \(h_{k,m} \in \{0,1\}\) — HAWB \(k\) assigned to MAWB \(m\).
  \item \(y_{f,u,m}^c\) — MAWB \(m\) on flight \(f\), ULD type \(u\), contract \(c\).
  \item Rate tier and pivot weight apply on aggregate MAWB chargeable weight
        \(\text{CW}_m = \sum_{k} h_{k,m} \cdot cw_k\) (plus dunnage factor), not per HAWB.
\end{itemize}
This is structurally the LCL pattern from \texttt{model/ocean\_lcl\_routing.tex}:
shipments \(\to\) consol unit \(\to\) physical transport leg. The v2 draft will
mirror the LCL formulation.

\subsection{Physical Network Graph \texorpdfstring{$G(N, A)$}{G(N,A)}}

The air freight network is a directed graph $G = (N, A)$.

\[
  N = N_O \cup N_{\text{CFS,O}} \cup N_{\text{AP,O}} \cup N_{\text{AP,H}} \cup N_{\text{AP,D}} \cup N_{\text{CFS,D}} \cup N_D
\]

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.4cm, labelwidth=3.2cm, style=nextline]
  \item[$N_O$ — Origins] One per shipper origin door (e.g., factory, warehouse). Commodity source nodes.
  \item[$N_{\text{CFS,O}}$ — Origin CFS] Container Freight Stations near origin airports
        (e.g., TPE-CFS, KHH-CFS). ULD build-up location. Airline-owned empty ULDs are
        delivered here; cargo from one or more shipments is stuffed into a ULD; built ULD
        is trucked to the airline cargo terminal.
  \item[$N_{\text{AP,O}}$ — Origin airports] Airline cargo terminals at departure airports
        (e.g., TPE, KHH). Built ULDs are received here for loading onto aircraft.
  \item[$N_{\text{AP,H}}$ — Hub airports] Intermediate airports on multi-hop routes
        (e.g., HKG, NRT, ANC). Through-cargo handling or re-ULDing occurs here.
  \item[$N_{\text{AP,D}}$ — Destination airports] Airline cargo terminals at arrival airports
        (e.g., JFK, ORD, LAX). ULDs are unloaded here.
  \item[$N_{\text{CFS,D}}$ — Destination CFS] CFS near destination airport for ULD breakdown.
        Trucked from airline cargo terminal; ULDs broken down; individual consignments
        released for final delivery.
  \item[$N_D$ — Destinations] One per consignee delivery door. Commodity sink nodes.
\end{description}

\[
  A = A_{\text{PU}} \cup A_{\text{OC}} \cup A_{\text{AIR}} \cup A_{\text{DC}} \cup A_{\text{FD}}
\]

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.4cm, labelwidth=3.2cm, style=nextline]
  \item[$A_{\text{PU}}$ — Pickup] $(i,j)$, $i \in N_O$, $j \in N_{\text{CFS,O}}$.
        Truck pickup from origin door to origin CFS.
  \item[$A_{\text{OC}}$ — Origin CFS to origin airport] $(i,j)$, $i \in N_{\text{CFS,O}}$,
        $j \in N_{\text{AP,O}}$. Built ULDs trucked from CFS to airline cargo terminal.
        Includes fixed CFS build-up time as part of effective arc duration.
  \item[$A_{\text{AIR}}$ — Flight legs] $(i,j)$, $i, j \in N_{\text{AP,O}} \cup N_{\text{AP,H}}
        \cup N_{\text{AP,D}}$. One arc per scheduled flight leg (specific carrier, flight
        number, departure date, origin airport, destination airport).
        A multi-stop flight (e.g., $f$: TPE$\to$ANC$\to$JFK) decomposes into two air arcs
        sharing the same flight number metadata.
  \item[$A_{\text{DC}}$ — Destination airport to destination CFS] $(i,j)$, $i \in N_{\text{AP,D}}$,
        $j \in N_{\text{CFS,D}}$. Trucked from airline cargo terminal to CFS.
        Includes fixed CFS breakdown time.
  \item[$A_{\text{FD}}$ — Final delivery] $(i,j)$, $i \in N_{\text{CFS,D}}$, $j \in N_D$.
        Trucked from destination CFS to consignee door.
\end{description}

\begin{remark}[Origin and destination CFS as explicit nodes]
Unlike ocean (where the POL terminal is one node), air introduces a mandatory CFS step at
origin (ULD build-up) and destination (ULD breakdown). Modeling CFS as an explicit node
permits: (a) the fixed CFS dwell time to enter feasibility checks, (b) future modeling of
CFS dock capacity, ULD availability at CFS, and (c) alternative-CFS routing (e.g., a
Kaohsiung shipment trucked to TPE-CFS for consolidation).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Flight legs vs.\ flight numbers]
A real-world flight number (e.g., 5Y9123 TPE$\to$ANC$\to$JFK) operates one aircraft sortie
with an intermediate stop. We model this as \emph{two arcs} in $A_{\text{AIR}}$ — $(\text{TPE},
\text{ANC})$ and $(\text{ANC}, \text{JFK})$ — both tagged with the same flight number. This
preserves transit time visibility at each leg and provides the structural hook for per-leg
ULD allocation accounting. The fact that the ULD physically stays on the aircraft across the
intermediate stop is captured by the through-ULD compatibility parameter $\psi$
(Section~\ref{sec:through-uld}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Commodity-Specific Subgraph \texorpdfstring{$G(N_k, A_k)$}{G(Nk,Ak)}}
\label{sec:subgraph}

The MILP for commodity $k$ operates on a subgraph $G(N_k, A_k) \subseteq G(N, A)$ containing
only arcs that (i) are time-feasible for commodity $k$, (ii) lie on at least one complete
path from $o(k)$ to $d(k)$, and (iii) are compatible with the commodity's cargo type and
ULD constraints (e.g., hazmat-restricted flights excluded for non-DGR-certified carriers).
See Section~\ref{sec:prefilter} for the construction algorithm.

\paragraph{Cargo ready window.}
The shipper offers cargo for pickup within a window
$[t_k^{\text{rdy,early}},\, t_k^{\text{rdy,late}}]$, not at a single moment. The model
treats $t_k(o(k))$ --- the actual pickup time at the origin door --- as a continuous
decision variable bounded by:
\begin{equation}
  t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} \;\leq\; t_k(o(k)) \;\leq\; t_k^{\text{rdy,late}}
  \label{eq:cargo-ready-window}
\end{equation}
The optimizer chooses the pickup time within the window. Anchors throughout the rest of
the model (subgraph optimistic arrival, big-M tightening, SLA computation) reference
$t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$ as the canonical ``cargo first available'' time, since
service-level commitments are measured from the shipper's earliest readiness, not from
when the forwarder elects to pick up.

\paragraph{Chargeable weight.}
\begin{equation}
  cw_k \defeq \max\!\left(w_k,\ v_k \cdot 167\right)
  \label{eq:chargeable}
\end{equation}
IATA standard: $1\,\text{m}^3 = 167\,\text{kg}$ volumetric equivalent. Dense cargo (machinery,
electronics) rated on $w_k$; bulky-light cargo (furniture, packaging) rated on volumetric.

\paragraph{Sourcing $\delta^{\text{cust}}_k$ and $\delta^{\text{rel}}_k$.}
For MVP, both parameters resolve from tenant-configurable lookup tables keyed on the
shipment attributes:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $\delta^{\text{cust}}_k = \text{lookup}^{\text{cust}}(\text{destination\_country}, \text{cargo\_type}, \text{commodity\_class})$
  \item $\delta^{\text{rel}}_k = \text{lookup}^{\text{rel}}(\text{release\_type})$
\end{itemize}
Initial values are tenant-calibrated from historical clearance times observed in the
forwarder's own broker performance data. The customs filing deadlines themselves
(AMS / ICS2 / ACI) are already modeled as effective cutoffs in
\S\ref{sec:flight-params} (Eq.~\ref{eq:effective-cutoff}); $\delta^{\text{cust}}_k$
captures the \emph{post-arrival} clearance hold, distinct from the pre-flight filing.

\paragraph{Effective transit times on each ground arc type.}
\begin{align}
  \tilde{\mu}_{ij}^{\text{PU}}  &= \mu_{ij}^{\text{PU}}
                                 & & \text{(pickup; no CFS dwell)} \label{eq:eff-transit-pu} \\
  \tilde{\mu}_{ij}^{\text{OC}}  &= \mu_{ij}^{\text{OC}} + \beta^{\text{O}}
                                 & & \text{(origin CFS $\to$ airport; includes build-up)} \label{eq:eff-transit-oc} \\
  \tilde{\mu}_{ij,k}^{\text{DC}} &= \mu_{ij}^{\text{DC}} + \delta^{\text{cust}}_k + \beta^{\text{D}}
                                 & & \text{(airport $\to$ destination CFS; includes customs + breakdown)} \label{eq:eff-transit-dc} \\
  \tilde{\mu}_{ij,k}^{\text{FD}} &= \mu_{ij}^{\text{FD}} + \delta^{\text{rel}}_k
                                 & & \text{(destination CFS $\to$ final delivery; includes AWB release)} \label{eq:eff-transit-fd}
\end{align}
Origin-side transit times $\tilde{\mu}^{\text{PU}}, \tilde{\mu}^{\text{OC}}$ are arc-only
(do not depend on $k$). Destination-side $\tilde{\mu}^{\text{DC}}_{ij,k}, \tilde{\mu}^{\text{FD}}_{ij,k}$
are shipment-specific because the clearance and release dwells depend on shipment
attributes.

\paragraph{Why the destination dwells are not deferred to P1.}
Without $\delta^{\text{cust}}_k$, the optimizer treats destination customs as
instantaneous --- door-to-door SLAs $T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ will be quoted tighter than
achievable. Without $\delta^{\text{rel}}_k$, the optimizer ignores the operational
reality that an \texttt{original\_awb\_surrender} shipment cannot release to consignee
until the AWB physically arrives, which often dwarfs the truck transit. Both terms are
modest in complexity (each adds one constant per shipment to the existing $\tilde{\mu}$
expressions) and large in operational impact, so both enter MVP.

\paragraph{Cutoff set and effective cutoff (per shipment).}
The cutoff set depends on the shipment's cargo type $\ctype_k$ and the destination region.
Define for shipment $k$ on flight $f$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{CO}_{f,k} \;\defeq\; \{\,\text{CGC}_{f,\ctype_k},\; \text{DCO}_f\,\} \cup
    \begin{cases}
      \{\text{AMS}_f\} & \text{if } d(k) \in \text{US destinations} \\
      \{\text{ICS2}_f\} & \text{if } d(k) \in \text{EU destinations} \\
      \{\text{ACI}_f\} & \text{if } d(k) \in \text{Canada destinations} \\
      \emptyset & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cutoff-set}
\end{equation}
Cargo and document preparation must complete before the \emph{most restrictive} of
these. The effective cutoff is the minimum:
\begin{equation}
  \text{CO}_{f,k}^* \;\defeq\; \min_{c \in \text{CO}_{f,k}}\, c
  \label{eq:effective-cutoff}
\end{equation}
Typical timing (offsets from $\text{ETD}_f$, indicative): GEN cutoff $\text{CGC}_{f,\text{GEN}}$
= ETD$-4$h to $-6$h; DGR cutoff $\text{CGC}_{f,\text{DGR}}$ = ETD$-6$h to $-12$h (earliest);
PER and AVI typically $\sim$ETD$-6$h. $\text{DCO}_f$ = ETD$-4$h to $-6$h; $\text{AMS}_f$ =
ETD$-4$h to $-5$h; $\text{ICS2}_f, \text{ACI}_f$ = ETD$-4$h. Exact values come from each
carrier's published cargo cutoff schedule; for MVP synthetic instances, populate from a
per-(carrier, $\tau$) lookup table.

\paragraph{Per-shipment document preparation time.}
Filing the MAWB+HAWB stack is not instant; it depends on shipment complexity:
\begin{equation}
  \text{prep\_time}_k \;\defeq\; \tau^{\text{base}} + \alpha \cdot |\text{HAWBs}_k|
    + \mathbb{1}[\ctype_k = \text{DGR}] \cdot \tau^{\text{DGR}}
    + \mathbb{1}[\ctype_k = \text{PER}] \cdot \tau^{\text{PER}}
    + \tau^{\text{customs}}(k)
  \label{eq:prep-time}
\end{equation}
Defaults: \(\tau^{\text{base}} = 30\) min, \(\alpha = 1.5\) min per HAWB,
\(\tau^{\text{DGR}} = 3\) h, \(\tau^{\text{PER}} = 1\) h, \(\tau^{\text{customs}}\)
0--2 h based on customs complexity. All parameterizable per tenant.

The binding feasibility check at the origin airport node \(a \in N_{\text{AP,O}}\) is:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_k(a) + \text{prep\_time}_k \;\leq\; \text{CO}_{f,k}^*
  \label{eq:cutoff-feasibility}
\end{equation}
This is enforced in subgraph construction (Section~\ref{sec:prefilter}) and in
constraint P.11. The shipment-indexed $\text{CO}_{f,k}^*$ captures both the cargo-type
dependency (DGR cutoff is materially tighter than GEN on most carriers) and the
destination-region dependency (US, EU, Canada add pre-load filings).

\subsection{ULD Specifications}
\label{sec:uld-spec}

ULD specifications are governed by IATA ULD Regulations (ULDR).\footnote{IATA Unit Load
Device Regulations (ULDR). \url{https://www.iata.org/en/publications/manuals/uld-regulations/}}
The model needs a richer per-ULD attribute set than vol/weight alone — physical fit at
the aircraft door, floor-load tolerance, deck position, tare-vs-cargo weight separation,
and certification all influence whether a given (shipment, flight, ULD) triple is
operationally feasible.

\paragraph{IATA ULD identification format.}
The standard ULD identifier is \texttt{XXX-NNNNN-CC}: 3 leading letters (type code),
4--5 numerals (serial number), 2 trailing letters (owning airline IATA code).\footnote{ULD CARE,
``ULD Identification Codes Demystified.''
\url{https://www.uldcare.com/articles/library/care/uld-identification-codes-demystified/}}
The 3-letter type code has structural meaning:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{1st letter}: family. \texttt{P} = Pallet, \texttt{A} = Aircraft container
        (rigid walls), \texttt{R} = Reefer, \texttt{U} = Non-certified.
  \item \textbf{2nd letter}: base dimensions. \texttt{M} = 96\textquotedbl{}\(\times\)125\textquotedbl{}
        (main-deck size), \texttt{K} = 60.4\textquotedbl{}\(\times\)61.5\textquotedbl{},
        \texttt{L} = 60.4\textquotedbl{}\(\times\)125\textquotedbl{}.
  \item \textbf{3rd letter}: contour / shape. \texttt{C} = standard cube,
        \texttt{E} = contoured, \texttt{P} = lower-deck contour.
\end{itemize}
Example: \textbf{PMC} = Pallet, 96\textquotedbl{}\(\times\)125\textquotedbl{} base, cube
contour. A flat aluminum pallet $244 \times 318$ cm with cargo stacked on top and
secured by net; PMCs are main-deck freighter ULDs. Contrast \textbf{LD3 (AKE/AVE)}:
rigid-walled container used in wide-body belly compartments, cargo stuffed inside
through a door. The distinction matters for loadability (pallets lose volume to net
gaps and contour) and aircraft compatibility (PMCs only fit freighters and main decks;
LD3s fit both passenger belly and freighter lower decks).

Fill rate cap & $\eta$ & Practical max fraction of $V_u$ usable due to nets, cargo shape (default $0.97$) & --- \\
Build-up time & $\beta^{u,\text{up}}$ & Forwarder labor minutes to build a $u$-type ULD & min \\
Breakdown time & $\beta^{u,\text{down}}$ & Minutes to break down at destination CFS & min \\
ULDR certification & --- & IATA ULDR certification level (standard / supplementary) & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Floor-load constraint.}
Each ULD position has a floor-load limit $\phi_u$ (typically $\sim 730$ kg/m\textsuperscript{2}
for standard ULDs).\footnote{Hansatic Air Freight ULD Guide, op.\ cit.\ Floor-load limits
vary by ULD type and by aircraft floor structure; the value used is the binding minimum
of the ULD limit and the aircraft-position limit.} A shipment with a heavy piece on a
small footprint (e.g., a 1{,}500 kg machine on a 0.8 m\textsuperscript{2} base produces
$\sim 1{,}875$ kg/m\textsuperscript{2}, well above limit) is operationally infeasible on
that ULD even if the aggregate weight fits. MVP captures this via the pre-processing
exclusion P.17 (physical fit), with the floor-load check incorporated alongside the
contour fit check.

\paragraph{Aircraft compatibility.}
Which ULD types fit which aircraft, at which position, is captured by:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $U_f \subseteq U$: ULD types acceptable on flight $f$. Derived from $\text{ac}(f)$
        and the published aircraft-ULD compatibility matrix (e.g., 777F accepts PMC main
        deck + AKE/AKH/AAY lower deck; A330 belly accepts AKE/AKH only).
  \item $P_{f,u}$: number of $u$-type ULD positions on $\text{ac}(f)$. An upper bound on
        physical positions, independent of the forwarder's contracted allocation. Used
        in P.5 (Aircraft Physical Position Cap).
\end{itemize}
Aircraft-ULD compatibility is published by manufacturers and codified per airline; for
MVP it is loaded as a static reference table per aircraft type.

\paragraph{Cargo-type compatibility (cross-references).}
Specific cargo-type acceptance on a given ULD is governed by separate parameters
elsewhere in the model:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Dangerous goods acceptance --- handled at flight level via $\text{dgr}(f)$
        (\S\ref{sec:flight-params}) and lithium-specific acceptance matrix
        (\S\ref{sec:lithium}).
  \item Cold chain --- temperature control is a P1 deferred concern; relevant ULD
        attribute is \texttt{pressurized} (above) for sensitive perishables.
  \item Class 7 radioactive position restrictions --- specific IATA DGR positions; P1.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Procurement Types and Supply Layer Catalog}
\label{sec:procurement-types}

The forwarder procures air capacity through several structurally distinct channels.
The rate-function generalization (Section~\ref{sec:rate-function}) handles
\emph{pricing} differences across channels via per-contract piecewise-linear
functions. This section catalogs the \emph{structural} differences: who holds the
allocation, who issues the MAWB, how allocations are accounted, and whether the
procurement decision is at the MAWB or HAWB level.

\paragraph{Supply type enum.}
Each contract \(c \in C\) carries a \(\text{supply\_type}(c)\) attribute:

  \text{supply\_type}(c) \in \{\,\texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_HARD},\;
    \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_SOFT},\;
    \texttt{GSA},\;
    \texttt{COLOADER},\;
    \texttt{DYNAMIC\_SPOT}\,\}
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Supply types catalog.}
\begin{center}

\paragraph{Mapping to existing parameter blocks.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Rate function \(R_c(\text{CW}_m)\) (Section~\ref{sec:rate-function}):
        applies to all types. Each supply type populates the
        \((b_i, m_i)\) tuples differently.
  \item Per-flight allocation \(\text{alloc}(c, f, u)\) and period allocation
        \(\text{alloc}(c, u, t)\) (Section~\ref{sec:bsa-params}): apply to
        \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_HARD}, \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_SOFT}, \texttt{GSA}.
        For \texttt{COLOADER} and \texttt{DYNAMIC\_SPOT}: not applicable
        (\(\text{alloc} = \infty\) effective).
  \item Take-or-pay minimum \(\underline{M}_{c,u,t}\) (Section~\ref{sec:bsa-params}):
        nonzero only for \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_HARD}.
  \item Pivot weight \(\pi_{c,u}\) (Section~\ref{sec:bsa-params}): only meaningful for
        \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_HARD} and \texttt{DIRECT\_BSA\_SOFT} when the contract
        rates per-ULD.
  \item Rate validity \(\text{expiry}_c\): only meaningful for
        \texttt{DYNAMIC\_SPOT}; ignored elsewhere.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Co-loader as structural dual-mode.}
\texttt{COLOADER} procurement is the only type where the tenant does \emph{not}
issue its own MAWB — instead, the tenant hands HAWBs to a co-loader (a larger
forwarder, e.g., K+N, DSV, Geodis) who consolidates them under their own MAWB.
The tenant is effectively a HAWB-shipper on someone else's MAWB. This affects
the v2 MAWB-centric formulation: tenant MAWB-level decision variables
\(y_{f,u,m}^c\) (Section~\ref{sec:mawb-hawb}) do not apply to co-loader-routed
HAWBs. Instead, introduce a HAWB-level binary:
\begin{equation}
  \text{coloader}_{k, o} \in \{0, 1\}: \text{HAWB } k \text{ uses co-loader option } o \in O_{\text{COLOADER}}
  \label{eq:coloader}
\end{equation}
with exactly-one constraint across procurement paths per HAWB:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_{m \in M} h_{k, m} \;+\; \sum_{o \in O_{\text{COLOADER}}} \text{coloader}_{k, o} \;=\; 1
\end{equation*}
The co-loader handles all downstream routing, ULD selection, and consolidation
opaque to the model. Cost: lose density-mixing visibility into co-loader's
consolidation, but gain a procurement path that mid-market forwarders without
direct carrier paper rely on heavily. Full formalization is part of the v2 MAWB
restructure (Section~\ref{sec:deferred}).

\paragraph{GSA as marked-up direct contract.}
\texttt{GSA} contracts are structurally identical to direct BSAs: forwarder issues
MAWB, GSA holds the allocation, MILP machinery applies unchanged. The only
difference is the rate function shape — GSAs typically add a 3--8\% markup on the
underlying carrier rate, encoded directly in the \((b_i, m_i)\) tuples. No new
constraints or variables; \texttt{GSA} is a tag on the existing contract entity.

\paragraph{Dynamic spot as single-segment with TTL.}
\texttt{DYNAMIC\_SPOT} contracts have:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Rate function: single segment \(\{(0, \text{quoted\_rate})\}\) + optional
        minimum charge.
  \item No allocation (\(\text{alloc} = \infty\)).
  \item Rate validity attribute \(\text{expiry}_c\): planning horizon must end
        before \(\text{expiry}_c\) (validated at the data layer).
\end{itemize}
Capacity availability uncertainty (RQ / KK / NS booking responses) is a separate
scope decision (\texttt{booking confirmation} modeling; deferred to a later point).

\paragraph{Out of MVP scope.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Charter capacity} — full-aircraft buyout, edge case for mid-market
        forwarders; defer.
  \item \textbf{Broker-of-record / freight-forwarder-as-customer} — multi-level
        forwarding hierarchies (forwarder buying from another forwarder buying from
        a co-loader); defer, model treats each tier as a separate co-loader
        relationship.
  \item \textbf{Alliance slot sharing} (\texttt{SkyTeam}, \texttt{Star Alliance},
        \texttt{oneworld}): cross-carrier capacity exchange; already deferred in
        Section~\ref{sec:deferred}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Period commitments are upstream]
$\underline{M}_{c,u,t}$ is computed by a separate upstream model that allocates how much of
each hard BSA's monthly take-or-pay commitment should be met by each routing run within the
period. The routing MILP takes $\underline{M}_{c,u,t}$ as a fixed input per run. The MILP
does \emph{not} simultaneously optimize period-level commitment allocation; that is a
higher-level decision.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Pivot weight semantics]
The pivot $\pi_{c,u}$ is the minimum chargeable weight per ULD per flight regardless of actual
cargo loaded. If a forwarder claims one LD3 with pivot $1{,}000\,\text{kg}$ on a flight but
loads only $400\,\text{kg}$ of cargo, the forwarder pays for $1{,}000\,\text{kg} \cdot r_c$.
This is the take-or-pay floor on hard BSAs.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Period boundary convention]
\label{rem:bsa-period-boundary}
A flight's membership in period $t$ for the period-utilization sums P.6, P.7 is
determined by the \textbf{UTC calendar month} of $\text{ETD}_f$ (consistent with
\S\ref{sec:tz-convention} time-zone convention). A flight with $\text{ETD}_f$ on
May 31 23:30 UTC counts in May; one on June 1 00:30 UTC counts in June. This
convention is normative by default; individual BSA contracts may specify an
alternative (e.g., origin-local-time month, ETA-destination month, week-Monday
boundary) as a per-contract override $\text{period\_basis}_c \in
\{\text{utc\_month}, \text{origin\_local\_month}, \text{etd\_week\_monday}, \ldots\}$
stored on the contract record. The default UTC-month avoids billing disputes from
ambiguity; tenant-specific overrides are honored when explicitly set in the
contract. Research did not surface a universal industry convention; per-contract
explicit choice is the safe play.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Spot rate cost per shipment.}
For shipment $k$ tendered on flight $f$ at the spot rate, the cost is the minimum across
applicable tiers:
\begin{equation}
  \text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f) \defeq \min_{b \in B}\, r^{\text{sp}}_{f,b} \cdot \max(cw_k,\, b)
  \label{eq:spot-cost}
\end{equation}

Since $cw_k$ is a parameter (known at planning time), $\text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f)$ is
pre-computable. The IATA rule that ``charging at the next tier with a lower rate may yield a
lower total cost'' is captured automatically by the min.

\subsection{Surcharge Parameters}
\label{sec:surcharge}

Real air-freight quotes layer 10--20 surcharges per shipment per arc on top of the base
freight rate. Fuel surcharge (FSC) and security surcharge (SSC) are the most visible,
but the full stack also includes terminal handling, customs filing (AMS / ICS2 / ACI),
AWB issuance, screening, DGR handling, war risk, peak-season surcharge, perishable /
cool-chain handling, valuables handling, overweight surcharge, and lithium handling.
Together these typically add 30--50\% on top of the base rate for a tier~2 forwarder
quote; omitting them produces systematic under-quoting.

\paragraph{Storage.}
Surcharges are stored in the \texttt{surcharge\_catalog} table per tenant
(\texttt{data\_model.md} \S6), versioned via the same append-only + per-run-snapshot
pattern as policy rules (\S4) and spot rate snapshots (\S5). Each catalog row carries:
\texttt{surcharge\_type} (enumerated), \texttt{scope} (applicability JSONB:
carrier, origin, destination, ac\_type, cargo\_type, region, date range), and
\texttt{calculation} (basis: per\_kg / per\_shipment / per\_awb / per\_uld / per\_cbm /
per\_piece / percent\_of\_freight, rate, currency, optional min/max).
See \texttt{data\_model.md} \S6.2 for the full type enumeration and \S6.4 for the
calculation formulas per basis.

\paragraph{Per-arc resolution.}
Pre-solve, for each shipment $k$ and each arc $(i,j) \in A_k$, the resolver materializes
the applicable surcharges:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\text{arc}}(k, i, j) \;\defeq\; \{\, s \in \text{surcharge\_catalog} :
    \text{scope\_match}(s, k, i, j) \,\wedge\, s.\text{status} = \text{active} \,\wedge\,
    \text{now} \in [s.\text{effective\_from}, s.\text{effective\_to})\,\}
  \label{eq:surcharge-applicable}
\end{equation}
The per-arc surcharge cost is the sum of resolved amounts:
\begin{equation}
  \text{surcharge\_cost}(k, i, j) \;\defeq\; \sum_{s \in S_{\text{arc}}(k, i, j)}
    \text{amount}(s, k, i, j)
  \label{eq:surcharge-cost}
\end{equation}
where $\text{amount}(s, k, i, j)$ is computed per the surcharge's calculation basis and
clipped by the optional $[\text{min\_charge}, \text{max\_charge}]$ band. Surcharges
split into two cost paths based on whether the basis can be attributed per-shipment
per-arc (path A) or only per-flight (path B):

\paragraph{Path A --- per-shipment-per-arc bases (resolve into $\text{surcharge\_cost}(k,i,j)$).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{per\_kg}: $\text{rate} \cdot cw_k$ (or $w_k$ if \texttt{weight\_basis = "actual"})
  \item \texttt{per\_shipment}: $\text{rate}$ (flat)
  \item \texttt{per\_awb}: $\text{rate}$ (flat per AWB; for a single-HAWB shipment, equivalent to per\_shipment)
  \item \texttt{per\_cbm}: $\text{rate} \cdot v_k$
  \item \texttt{per\_piece}: $\text{rate} \cdot \text{piece\_count}_k$
  \item \texttt{percent\_of\_freight}: $\text{rate} \cdot \text{base\_freight}_k$
        (only when \texttt{percent\_of\_field = base\_freight}; \texttt{base\_freight}
        is pre-computed per arc from rate tables, a constant)
\end{itemize}
Because $cw_k$, $v_k$, $w_k$, $\text{piece\_count}_k$, and $\text{base\_freight}_k$ are
known constants, $\text{surcharge\_cost}(k, i, j)$ for Path-A surcharges is a constant
once resolved --- not a decision variable.

\paragraph{Path B --- flight-level bases (resolve into $\text{flight\_uld\_surcharge\_cost}(f)$).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{per\_uld}: $\text{rate} \cdot \sum_{u, c} z_{f,u}^c$ --- linear in the
        integer variable $z$; charged once per flight per loaded ULD, regardless of
        which shipments share those ULDs. Captured as a separate objective term, not
        multiplied by $x_{ij}^k$.
\end{itemize}
The flight-level cost is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{flight\_uld\_surcharge\_cost}(f) \;\defeq\;
  \sum_{s \in S_{\text{flight}}(f)}\, \text{rate}_s \cdot \sum_{u \in U_f,\, c \in C_f} z_{f,u}^c
  \label{eq:flight-uld-surcharge}
\end{equation}
where $S_{\text{flight}}(f)$ is the set of \texttt{per\_uld}-basis surcharges
applicable to flight $f$. Operationally: terminal-handling-per-ULD, airline-charged
ULD build-up fees, and similar costs depend on how many ULDs were loaded by the
forwarder, not which shipments contributed cargo to them.

\paragraph{Why the split.}
Treating \texttt{per\_uld} as per-shipment-per-arc was a bug in the prior draft:
multiplying $\text{rate} \cdot \sum_u z_{f,u}^c$ by $x_{ij}^k$ either over-charges
(every shipment on the flight pays the full fee, $n$ times for $n$ shipments) or
introduces a bilinear $x \cdot z$ term requiring McCormick linearization. The
flight-level treatment is correct accounting, eliminates the bilinearity, and
matches \texttt{data\_model.md} \S6.5 ``two cost paths'' resolution.

\paragraph{Common ground-arc surcharges.}
Most surcharges apply to specific arc types:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Origin THC, AWB fee, origin screening, DGR handling: origin-side arcs ($A_k^{\text{PU}} \cup A_k^{\text{OC,origin-leg}}$)
  \item Destination THC, AMS / ICS2 / ACI, destination handling: destination-side arcs ($A_k^{\text{DC}} \cup A_k^{\text{FD}}$)
  \item FSC, SSC, war risk, PSS / GRI, percent\_of\_freight surcharges: air arcs ($A_k^{\text{AIR}}$)
\end{itemize}
The \texttt{applies\_on\_arc\_types} scope field controls this gating; the resolver
attributes the cost to the matching arcs only.

For a 1{,}200 kg GEN shipment TPE $\to$ JFK on CX564 + CX880 (PRM\_AIR\_EXP, May 2026

\midrule

SSC (0.18/kg) & 216 & 216 \\
THC origin & 65 & --- \\

\subsection{Hub Connection Parameters (Through-ULD vs.\ Re-ULDing)}
\label{sec:through-uld}

For each pair of consecutive air arcs $(f, g)$ at hub $j$ — meaning $f$ ends at $j$ and $g$
starts at $j$ — and each ULD type $u$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7cm}l}
\toprule
Parameter & Symbol & Description & Unit \\
\midrule
Through-ULD compatible & $\psi_{f,g,u}$ & 1 if a ULD of type $u$ can transit hub $j$ without being broken down (same aircraft for through-flights; or same airline through-cargo agreement) & boolean \\
MCT (through) & $\text{MCT}_{f,g}^{\text{thru}}$ & Min connect time when through-ULD is used (technical-stop turnaround or hub through-cargo time) & hours \\
MCT (re-ULD) & $\text{MCT}_{f,g}^{\text{reULD}}$ & Min connect time when ULD must be broken down and rebuilt at hub & hours \\
Rehandling cost (same-ULD) & $\rho_{f,g,u}^{\text{reULD}}$ & Cost per ULD when same type $u$ is used on both legs but $\psi_{f,g,u}=0$ (e.g., interline pair without ULD interchange agreement) & USD/ULD \\
Rehandling cost (cross-ULD) & $\bar{\rho}_{f,g}^{\text{reULD}}$ & Cost when different ULD types are used on $f$ and $g$ (e.g., LD3 belly to PMC freighter); typically equal to or slightly higher than $\rho^{\text{reULD}}$ since cargo restuffs into a different shape & USD/ULD \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{defn}[Re-ULDing]
\emph{Re-ULDing} is the operation of breaking down a built ULD at an intermediate (hub)
airport and stuffing its cargo into a different ULD for the onward flight. It occurs when:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,label=(\alph*)]
  \item the two consecutive flights are operated by \emph{different airlines} (interline
        routing) — each airline owns a distinct ULD pool and ULDs cannot transfer;
  \item the two flights require \emph{different ULD types} (e.g., LD3 on a passenger belly
        leg and PMC main-deck pallet on a freighter leg);
  \item the hub lacks through-cargo facilities for this ULD type (rare at major hubs).
\end{enumerate}
Re-ULDing is operationally expensive: it adds $\sim 4$--$8$ hours of dwell time
(breakdown + storage + rebuild), incurs $\sim$\$150--\$500 in handling fees per ULD, and
introduces cargo damage risk. When $\psi_{f,g,u} = 1$, the ULD physically stays intact
across the hub — either remaining on the same aircraft (through-flight technical stop) or
moving as a complete unit between aircraft at hub through-cargo facilities.
\end{defn}

\paragraph{ULD interchange set.}
Let \(\Pi \subseteq \{\, (c_1, c_2, u) \,\}\) be the \emph{ULD interchange set}: triples
for which carriers \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) have a bilateral or alliance-level IATA ULD-CPM
(Container Pallet Movement) agreement covering ULD type \(u\). When
\((c_1, c_2, u) \in \Pi\), a built ULD of type \(u\) can physically transfer from \(c_1\)'s
pool to \(c_2\)'s pool at a hub with cross-airline transfer facilities --- no breakdown,
no rebuild, no fee. Typical sources for \(\Pi\):
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Star Alliance ULD pool: AC, LH, NH, OZ, SK, UA, ... covering common types (LD3, PMC, AKE).
  \item SkyTeam ULD pool: AF, KL, DL, KE, MU, ...
  \item oneworld ULD pool: AA, BA, CX, JL, QF, ...
  \item Bilateral non-alliance agreements (limited; e.g., cargo-only carrier pairs).
\end{itemize}
For MVP synthetic instances, \(\Pi\) is populated from a static table of alliance
memberships intersected with the ULD type catalog. For production, the source is each
airline's published ULD interchange directory or the IATA ULD-CPM database.

\paragraph{MVP rule for pre-computing $\psi_{f,g,u}$.}
$\psi_{f,g,u}$ is a \emph{pre-computed parameter} populated before solve. The rule keys
on the \emph{operating} carrier $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(\cdot)$, not the marketing
carrier --- ULD pool ownership follows the airline that physically operates the aircraft,
not the airline whose code appears on the AWB. Set $\psi_{f,g,u} = 1$ if and only if
$u \in U_f \cap U_g$ \emph{and} at least one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item[(a)] \textbf{Through-flight (technical stop).} $\text{fno}(f) = \text{fno}(g)$ \emph{and}
        $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) = \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(g)$. The ULD never
        leaves the aircraft.
  \item[(b)] \textbf{Same-airline through-cargo at hub.}
        $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) = \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(g)$ \emph{and} the hub
        $j$ supports through-cargo handling for type $u$ at this carrier's terminal.
  \item[(c)] \textbf{Interline ULD interchange.}
        $(\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f),\,\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(g),\,u) \in \Pi$
        \emph{and} the hub $j$ supports cross-airline ULD transfer for the relevant pool.
\end{itemize}
Otherwise $\psi_{f,g,u} = 0$ and re-ULDing is required.

\begin{remark}[Why $\psi$ is a parameter, not a decision variable --- and why this is the right MVP choice]
\label{rem:psi-as-param}
Whether a built ULD can ride through a hub is determined by physical and contractual
realities that exist before the forwarder plans the move:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Which carrier operates each leg (codeshares are irrelevant --- only the operator's
        ULD pool matters).
  \item Whether the hub has the through-cargo handling infrastructure for that ULD type.
  \item Whether a bilateral or alliance ULD-CPM agreement exists between the operators
        for that ULD type.
\end{itemize}
The forwarder cannot ``elect'' through-ULD where the operational reality doesn't allow it;
conversely, the forwarder generally has no operational reason to elect re-ULDing when
through-ULD is available --- it just adds dwell time and rehandling fees with no benefit.
The single edge case where the forwarder \emph{could} elect re-ULD despite $\psi = 1$
is hub re-sorting (splitting one ULD's cargo across multiple onward flights). That edge
case is rare in the mid-market forwarder context, structurally distinct, and deferred to
P1; see Section~\ref{sec:deferred}, item~\ref{item:psi-decision-var}.
\textbf{Bottom line for MVP:} $\psi$ is a data input. The model only needs the right data;
no decision-variable promotion is needed. If you (future-reader) are considering revisiting
this, the question is not ``should $\psi$ be a decision variable?'' --- the question is
``does the routing-with-resort use case justify the model complexity?'' For MVP, no.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Reading the rule with an example.}
Consider shipment $k$ routing TPE $\to$ JFK across three candidate hub connections, with
$u = $ PMC pallet:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \emph{Path A --- CX564 TPE-HKG + CX880 HKG-JFK (same airline).} Rule (b) applies:
        $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}} = \text{CX}$ on both legs; HKG has Cathay through-cargo
        for PMC. $\psi_{\text{CX564, CX880, PMC}} = 1$. Hub MCT $\approx 3$h, no rehandling fee.
  \item \emph{Path B --- AC2 TPE-YYZ + LH471 YYZ-JFK (Star Alliance interline).} Rule (c)
        applies: $(\text{AC}, \text{LH}, \text{PMC}) \in \Pi$ via Star Alliance pool;
        YYZ supports cross-airline transfer. $\psi = 1$. Hub MCT $\approx$ 3--4h, no
        rehandling fee. (A naïve ``same-carrier-only'' rule would incorrectly return
        $\psi = 0$ here and over-count re-ULDing on alliance interline routings ---
        this is the bug that motivated Task~\#6's correction.)
  \item \emph{Path C --- BR32 TPE-LAX + AA90 LAX-JFK (non-alliance interline).} Neither
        (b) nor (c) applies: BR is Star Alliance, AA is oneworld; no joint ULD pool.
        $\psi = 0$. Re-ULDing required at LAX: $\sim$6--12h dwell, $\sim$\$150--500/ULD fee.
\end{itemize}

The MILP uses $\psi$ in constraint P.14 (Section~\ref{sec:constraints}) to select the
applicable MCT, and in the objective (Section~\ref{sec:objective}) to attribute
rehandling cost.

\subsection{Embargo Parameters}
\label{sec:embargo}

Embargoes are carrier-, route-, commodity-, and time-bounded prohibitions on cargo
acceptance. They are imposed by carriers (operational policy, security incidents,
peak capacity), by regulators (geopolitical, sanctions), or by IATA / industry
notice. The model represents embargoes as a parameter set used at subgraph
construction, not as MILP constraints — the optimizer never sees embargoed arcs.

\paragraph{Embargo record schema.}
Let \(E\) be the set of embargo records. Each \(e \in E\) is a tuple:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9cm}}
\toprule
Field & Description \\
\midrule
\(\text{carrier}(e)\) & IATA carrier code, or \texttt{*} wildcard \\
\(\text{origin}(e)\) & Origin airport code, region prefix (e.g., \texttt{CN-*}), or \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{dest}(e)\) & Destination airport code, region prefix, or \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{hub}(e)\) & Transit hub airport code, or \(\emptyset\) (applies regardless of routing) \\
\(\text{ac\_type}(e)\) & \texttt{FREIGHTER} / \texttt{PAX\_BELLY} / \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{cargo\_type}(e)\) & \texttt{GEN} / \texttt{DGR} / \texttt{PER} / \texttt{VAL} / \texttt{AVI} / \texttt{HUM} / \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{commodity}(e)\) & 4-digit IATA commodity code, or \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{dgr\_class}(e)\) & DGR class+subclass (e.g., \texttt{9-II}), or \texttt{*} \\
\(\text{lithium\_pi}(e)\) & PI965 / PI966 / PI967 / PI968 / PI969 / PI970 / \(\emptyset\) (see Section~\ref{sec:lithium}) \\
\(\text{start}(e), \text{end}(e)\) & Date-time validity range \\
\(\text{source}(e)\) & \texttt{carrier\_notice} / \texttt{iata} / \texttt{regulatory} / \texttt{tenant\_internal} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Active embargoes for a flight.}
\begin{equation}
  E_f \;\defeq\; \{ e \in E : \text{start}(e) \leq \text{ETD}_f \leq \text{end}(e) \}
  \label{eq:active-embargoes}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Match predicate.}
Embargo record \(e\) matches the (commodity \(k\), flight \(f\)) pair, denoted
\(\text{match}(e, k, f)\), iff every non-wildcard field of \(e\) matches the
corresponding attribute of the shipment-flight pair:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item carrier and aircraft type from \(f\),
  \item origin and destination airports from \((o_{\text{AP}}(k), d_{\text{AP}}(k))\)
        or from arc endpoints,
  \item transit hub from the path through which \((k, f)\) connects (or \(\emptyset\) for
        any-routing records),
  \item cargo type / commodity / DGR class / lithium PI from \(k\)'s commodity
        attributes.
\end{itemize}
Wildcard fields in \(e\) match anything. Records in \(E_f\) OR together: a flight-arc
is embargoed for \(k\) iff \emph{any} record in \(E_f\) matches.

\paragraph{Arc embargo-feasibility.}
\begin{equation}
  \text{embargo\_ok}(k, f) \;\defeq\;
    \neg \, \exists\, e \in E_f : \text{match}(e, k, f)
  \label{eq:embargo-feasibility}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Examples (illustrative records).}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{10.5cm}}
\toprule
Type & Record \\
\midrule
Carrier-commodity & \(\text{carrier}=\texttt{CX},\, \text{origin}=\texttt{TPE},\, \text{dest}=\texttt{US-*},\, \text{hub}=\texttt{HKG},\, \text{lithium\_pi}=\texttt{PI965},\, \text{start}=2023\text{-}06\text{-}01,\, \text{end}=2099\text{-}12\text{-}31\) \\
Seasonal Hajj & \(\text{carrier}=\texttt{EK},\, \text{dest}=\texttt{JED},\, \text{cargo\_type}=\texttt{GEN},\, \text{start}=2026\text{-}06\text{-}10,\, \text{end}=2026\text{-}07\text{-}22\) \\

Aircraft-type DGR & \(\text{carrier}=\texttt{*},\, \text{ac\_type}=\texttt{PAX\_BELLY},\, \text{lithium\_pi}=\texttt{PI965},\, \text{start}=2024\text{-}04\text{-}01,\, \text{end}=2099\text{-}12\text{-}31\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Sourcing.}
For MVP, \(E\) is a manually-curated table maintained by tenant ops, seeded from
carrier embargo portal pages, IATA alerts (TACT supplements), and operational
knowledge. P1: agent capability to parse carrier notices (email, Slack) into
structured records with human review. P2: WebCargo / cargo.one embargo API if
exposed.

\paragraph{Scope decisions for MVP.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item All embargoes are hard prohibitions. Soft / capacity-driven restrictions
        are handled in the rate / capacity layer (Section~\ref{sec:bsa-params}), not as embargoes.
  \item Individual carriers only; alliance grouping (\texttt{SkyTeam}, \texttt{Star Alliance},
        \texttt{oneworld}) deferred to P1.
  \item Embargo set is global per tenant (carrier-imposed embargoes affect all
        shippers). Tenant-specific carrier exclusions are handled separately as
        carrier blacklist (Section~\ref{sec:deferred}, distinct from embargoes).
  \item Lithium PI codes referenced by \(\text{lithium\_pi}(e)\) are resolved by
        the lithium taxonomy in the next revision (separate scope decision).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lithium Battery Taxonomy and Per-Flight Acceptance}
\label{sec:lithium}

Lithium batteries account for an estimated 30--40\% of TPE/HKG eastbound air cargo
in 2026. They are not a single commodity class: the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations
distinguish six packing instructions and three section tiers per PI, and each carrier
publishes per-route acceptance rules that vary by PI, section, and aircraft type.
Modeling lithium as a generic ``DGR'' boolean conflates flights that accept
PI967-contained-in-equipment with those that refuse PI965 IA standalone cells.

\paragraph{IATA lithium taxonomy.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{4.2cm}l}
\toprule
UN \# & Chemistry & Configuration & PI \\
\midrule
UN3480 & Li-ion    & Standalone & PI965 \\
UN3481 & Li-ion    & Packed with equipment & PI966 \\
UN3481 & Li-ion    & Contained in equipment & PI967 \\
UN3090 & Li-metal  & Standalone & PI968 \quad (forbidden on passenger aircraft globally since 2015) \\
UN3091 & Li-metal  & Packed with equipment & PI969 \\
UN3091 & Li-metal  & Contained in equipment & PI970 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Within each PI, the IATA \emph{Section} tier determines regulatory burden:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2cm, labelwidth=1.8cm, style=nextline]
  \item[Section IA] Large cells / batteries (\(>100\) Wh Li-ion, \(>2\) g Li metal).
                    Fully regulated. Most restricted.
  \item[Section IB] \(\leq 100\) Wh Li-ion / \(\leq 2\) g Li metal in larger
                    packaging. Partial relief.
  \item[Section II] Small batteries below threshold quantities. Most operational
                    relief, still regulated.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Commodity lithium attributes.}
Extend the commodity parameter block with an optional \(\text{lithium\_spec}_k\)
record (\(\emptyset\) for non-lithium shipments):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9cm}}
\toprule
Field & Description \\
\midrule
\(\text{un\_number}\) & \texttt{UN3480} / \texttt{UN3481} / \texttt{UN3090} / \texttt{UN3091} \\
\(\text{pi\_code}\) & \texttt{PI965} / \texttt{PI966} / \texttt{PI967} / \texttt{PI968} / \texttt{PI969} / \texttt{PI970} \\
\(\text{section}\) & \texttt{IA} / \texttt{IB} / \texttt{II} \\
\(\text{watt\_hours}\) & Maximum Wh per battery in the shipment \\
\(\text{soc\_compliant}\) & Boolean: shipper-certified SOC \(\leq 30\%\) of rated capacity
                              (required for UN3480, UN3090 standalone) \\
\(\text{ddr}\) & Boolean: damaged / defective / recalled. Universally refused in MVP. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Per-flight acceptance matrix.}
For each flight \(f\) with aircraft type indicator
\(\text{ac\_type}(f) \in \{\texttt{FREIGHTER}, \texttt{PAX\_BELLY}\}\), the carrier
publishes an acceptance matrix:
\begin{equation}
  \text{lithium\_accept}_f \,:\, (\text{PI}, \text{Section}, \text{ac\_type}) \to \{\text{True}, \text{False}\}
  \label{eq:lithium-accept}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Whitelist semantics}: default is \texttt{False} (refuse). A flight accepts a
\((\text{PI}, \text{Section})\) combination only if the matrix explicitly says
\texttt{True}. The cost asymmetry (false-accept implies 48 h roll, fire-risk
liability) justifies a default-deny posture.

\paragraph{Lithium feasibility predicate.}
For shipment \(k\) with \(\text{lithium\_spec}_k \neq \emptyset\) and flight \(f\):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \text{lithium\_ok}(k, f) \;\defeq\;
    & \neg \, \text{lithium\_spec}_k.\text{ddr} \\
    \;\wedge\; & \bigl( \text{lithium\_spec}_k.\text{un\_number} \in \{\texttt{UN3480}, \texttt{UN3090}\}
                 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{lithium\_spec}_k.\text{soc\_compliant} \bigr) \\
    \;\wedge\; & \text{lithium\_accept}_f\!\bigl[\text{lithium\_spec}_k.\text{pi\_code},\,
                 \text{lithium\_spec}_k.\text{section},\, \text{ac\_type}(f)\bigr] = \text{True}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:lithium-ok}
\end{equation}
For non-lithium shipments, \(\text{lithium\_ok}(k, f) \equiv \text{True}\) trivially.

\paragraph{Interaction with embargoes.}
The whitelist acceptance matrix is the \emph{positive} statement of carrier
acceptance. The embargo set (Section~\ref{sec:embargo}) is the \emph{negative}
statement of carrier or regulatory refusal, possibly time-bounded. Both must hold:
a shipment-arc is lithium-feasible iff acceptance holds AND no active embargo
matches. Both predicates are AND-ed in subgraph construction
(Section~\ref{sec:prefilter}).

\paragraph{Aircraft-type dependency.}
Acceptance differs between freighter and passenger belly for the same carrier and
route. The matrix must retain at minimum this binary aircraft-type distinction
even if other aircraft-type detail is dropped from the model (parameter
\(\text{ac\_type}(f)\) is the minimum surviving granularity).

\paragraph{Scope decisions for MVP.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item All six PIs and all three sections supported.
  \item SOC compliance is shipper-certified boolean; verification deferred to P1.
  \item UN 38.3 test summary validation deferred to P1 (assumed valid in MVP).
  \item Per-flight aggregate lithium quantity caps (e.g., ``max 50 kg PI965 II per
        flight'') deferred to P1; binary shipment-level feasibility only in MVP.
  \item PI965 Section IA double-packaging requirements handled at CFS layer, not
        at routing.
  \item DDR (damaged / defective / recalled) batteries universally refused;
        specialized DDR charter routing deferred to P1.
  \item Out of scope entirely: battery recycling shipments (PI909), vehicle
        batteries (PI952), emerging chemistries (sodium-ion, solid-state — no
        standardized PI as of 2026).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sourcing.}
Each carrier publishes per-PI acceptance rules as an addendum to the IATA DGR.
For MVP, \(\text{lithium\_accept}_f\) is a manually-curated per-carrier table
seeded from carrier cargo portals and IATA DGR section 9 (state and operator
variations). Annual refresh plus event-driven updates after carrier policy
changes (typically following lithium fire incidents).

\paragraph{Scope note --- per-flight aggregate caps are carrier-side, not forwarder-side.}
Carriers publish per-flight aggregate limits on lithium quantity (e.g., max
\textit{N} kg of PI965~II per flight) and enforce these at acceptance across all
forwarders booking the same flight. A single forwarder cannot model the aggregate
because they do not see other forwarders' bookings on the same flight. The MILP's
job is correct \emph{per-shipment} declaration and acceptance check (modeled here);
aggregate enforcement is the carrier's responsibility. If a forwarder's shipment
is bumped at acceptance because the carrier's aggregate cap was reached after
booking, this surfaces as a booking-rejection event handled by the recovery
workflow (\S\ref{sec:locked-commitments}, booking-confirmation rejection recovery).

\subsection{Cargo Screening Certification (TSA CCSF / EU ACC3-RA3)}
\label{sec:screening}

After 9/11 (US) and the 2010 Yemen cargo-bomb plot (EU), regulators mandated that
cargo on passenger flights to / through US and EU airspace be screened (X-ray,
explosive trace detection, physical, or canine) prior to loading. Cargo not certified
through the appropriate chain at origin is refused at acceptance --- the optimizer's
recommendation is operationally undeliverable. This subsection adds the screening
gate to the subgraph filter.

\paragraph{Regulatory anchors.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{US} --- TSA Certified Cargo Screening Program (CCSP), 49 CFR Parts 1548
        and 1549. Cargo on US-destined passenger flights must be screened at a
        \emph{Certified Cargo Screening Facility (CCSF)}, OR shipped by a \emph{known
        shipper} with TSA-recognized security profile.\footnote{TSA, 49 CFR 1549
        Certified Cargo Screening Program; 49 CFR 1548 Indirect Air Carrier Security.}
        Freighter (all-cargo) flights operate under different rules and do not require
        the CCSF chain for general cargo.
  \item \textbf{EU} --- Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/1998, ACC3 / RA3 / KC3 program.
        Air cargo entering EU airspace must transit a validated chain: \emph{ACC3}
        (Air Cargo Carrier operating into the EU from a Third Country) accepts cargo
        only from an \emph{RA3} (Regulated Agent --- 3rd country) or directly from a
        \emph{KC3} (Known Consignor --- 3rd country).\footnote{Commission Implementing
        Regulation (EU) 2015/1998 laying down detailed measures for the implementation
        of the common basic standards on aviation security.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Shipment-side attribute.}
Each shipment carries $\text{screening\_status}_k$ (\texttt{shipment\_attributes.md}
\S2.7):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9cm}}
\toprule
Value & Meaning \\
\midrule
\texttt{known\_shipper} & TSA-recognized known shipper (US program); satisfies CCSP without per-shipment screening \\
\texttt{ccsf\_screened} & Screened at a CCSF facility (US); accepted for US PAX\_BELLY \\
\texttt{ra3\_kc3\_chain} & Transits RA3 or KC3 validated chain (EU); accepted for EU \\
\texttt{none} & Not screened / not in any program; accepted only for FREIGHTER routings \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Flight-side predicate.}
Whether a flight requires the screening chain depends on the destination and aircraft
type:
\begin{equation}
  \text{screening\_required}(f, d(k)) \;\defeq\;
    \mathbb{1}\bigl[\text{ac\_type}(f) = \texttt{PAX\_BELLY}\bigr] \cdot
    \mathbb{1}\bigl[\text{country}(d(k)) \in \mathcal{R}^{\text{screen}}\bigr]
  \label{eq:screening-required}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}^{\text{screen}}$ is the set of regulatory regions enforcing pre-load
screening for passenger-flight cargo. For MVP: $\mathcal{R}^{\text{screen}} = \{\text{US}, \text{EU},
\text{UK},\, \ldots\}$ --- tenant-configurable, with US and EU as the default-enforced
regions. Freighter flights are exempt (the model returns false regardless of
destination).

\paragraph{Subgraph feasibility predicate.}
For flight $f$ to be admitted to $A_k^{\text{AIR}}$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{screening\_ok}(k, f) \;\defeq\;
    \neg\, \text{screening\_required}(f, d(k))
    \;\vee\;
    \text{screening\_status}_k \in \text{accepted}\bigl(\text{country}(d(k))\bigr)
  \label{eq:screening-ok}
\end{equation}
where $\text{accepted}(\text{country})$ is the destination-region-specific acceptance
set:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item For US destinations: $\{\texttt{known\_shipper}, \texttt{ccsf\_screened}\}$
  \item For EU destinations: $\{\texttt{ra3\_kc3\_chain}\}$
  \item For other regulated destinations: per regulator-specific table
\end{itemize}
The predicate evaluates True when either (i) screening is not required for this flight
$\times$ destination, or (ii) the shipment's screening certification is in the accepted
set for that destination. Otherwise the flight is excluded from $A_k$ at subgraph
construction --- the optimizer never sees a routing it cannot operationally execute.

\paragraph{Multi-region routing.}
If routing crosses multiple regulated regions (e.g., a US-origin shipment connecting
through an EU hub to a non-regulated destination), the gate applies at the first
regulated leg's loading. The model's per-flight evaluation handles this correctly
because each flight is checked against its own destination requirement. A shipment
\texttt{ccsf\_screened} but not \texttt{ra3\_kc3\_chain} can fly TPE$\to$JFK on a US
PAX\_BELLY route, but cannot route TPE$\to$LHR$\to$JFK if the TPE-LHR leg requires
EU-chain certification.

\subsection{Locked Commitments and Execution State}
\label{sec:locked-commitments}

At any planning moment, the rolling-horizon optimizer is invoked over a window that
may contain shipments at various stages of execution. Pickup may already have
dispatched; cargo may sit at the origin CFS; AWBs may have been filed; ULDs may be
loaded on aircraft. Decisions \emph{already taken} cannot be unwound by the optimizer.
This subsection defines the lock semantics that preserve prior commitments while
leaving remaining decisions free.

\paragraph{Lifecycle states and lock semantics.}
The shipment lifecycle (defined in \texttt{data\_model.md}) is a 7-state DAG. Each
state maps to a lock posture:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{7.5cm}l}
\toprule
Lifecycle state & Lock semantics & MILP visibility \\
\midrule
\texttt{UNROUTED} & No commitments; fully open. & Full decision space \\
\texttt{SOFT\_PLANNED} & Tentative routing for cost preview; revocable. & Full decision space; current plan can be used as warm-start \\
\texttt{FIRM\_DEADLINE} & Dry-run window closed; about to commit. & Open if window not expired; near-locked otherwise \\
\texttt{FIRM\_PLANNED} & Routing committed to carriers; bookings issued. & Locked prefix = full intended route until execution events update it \\
\texttt{IN\_TRANSIT} & Cargo physically moving; legs lock as events arrive. & Locked prefix grows monotonically with execution events \\
\texttt{DESTINATION\_PLANNING} & All air legs done; destination-side ground legs still open. & Air arcs locked; destination CFS and final delivery arcs open \\
\texttt{DELIVERED} & Terminal state. & Excluded from MILP entirely \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Locked-commitment set $K^{\text{loc}}$ and locked prefix $A_k^{\text{loc}}$.}
$K^{\text{loc}} \subseteq K$ is the set of shipments with one or more locked decisions
in the current solve. For each $k \in K^{\text{loc}}$, the locked prefix
$A_k^{\text{loc}} \subseteq A_k$ collects the arcs committed to be used, and
$A_k^{\text{loc-off}}$ collects arcs explicitly committed \emph{not} to be used
(e.g., a candidate flight that was passed over in favor of the committed routing).
Decision-variable values along the locked prefix are pre-specified parameters:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Arc-use: $\bar{x}_{ij}^k \in \{0,1\}$ for each $(i,j) \in A_k^{\text{loc}} \cup A_k^{\text{loc-off}}$.
  \item ULD assignment: $\bar{y}_{f,u,k}^c \in \{0,1\}$ for each $(f, u, c)$ committed
        on a locked air arc.
  \item Spot booking: $\bar{b}_{f,k} \in \{0,1\}$ for each spot-rate flight already
        booked.
  \item Observed node arrival times: $\bar{t}_k(i) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ for nodes
        on the executed portion of the route (from milestone events such as
        \texttt{gate-out}, \texttt{cargo-loaded}, \texttt{flight-departed},
        \texttt{flight-arrived}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Per-shipment lock schema.}
Each shipment in $K^{\text{loc}}$ carries:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{7cm}l}
\toprule
Field & Description & Source \\
\midrule
\texttt{lifecycle\_state} & Current state in the 7-state DAG & system of record \\
\texttt{committed\_arcs} & Set of $(arc, value)$ tuples; $A_k^{\text{loc}} \cup A_k^{\text{loc-off}}$ derived from this & booking + execution events \\
\texttt{committed\_uld\_assignments} & $(f, u, c) \to \bar{y}$, per locked air arc & ULD build-up events \\
\texttt{committed\_spot\_bookings} & $f \to \bar{b}$, per booked spot flight & spot booking event \\
\texttt{observed\_node\_times} & node $\to \bar{t}_k(i)$, for executed nodes & milestone events \\
\texttt{lock\_horizon} & Datetime through which locks are enforced & rolling-horizon controller \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{How lock state is derived.}
Locks are not stored as a separate decision; they are derived at each solve from
(i)~the shipment's current lifecycle state and (ii)~execution events recorded since
the last booking. The mapping:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{FIRM\_PLANNED} $\to$ all booking-event arcs are locked (typically the
        full intended route until execution begins).
  \item \texttt{IN\_TRANSIT} $\to$ arcs are locked once their corresponding
        \texttt{gate-out} / \texttt{cargo-loaded} / \texttt{flight-departed} event is
        recorded; air arcs become irrevocable at \texttt{flight-departed}.
  \item \texttt{DESTINATION\_PLANNING} $\to$ all air arcs are locked (final flight
        arrived); destination CFS and final-delivery arcs remain free.
\end{itemize}
The rolling-horizon controller is responsible for materializing
\texttt{committed\_arcs}, \texttt{committed\_uld\_assignments},
\texttt{committed\_spot\_bookings}, and \texttt{observed\_node\_times} from these
signals before invoking the MILP.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
A Wednesday 14:00 planning window contains three shipments:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item $k_1$ --- \texttt{UNROUTED}: $k_1 \notin K^{\text{loc}}$. Full decision space.
  \item $k_2$ --- \texttt{IN\_TRANSIT}: pickup completed, cargo at TPE CFS, AWB filed
        on CI5232 TPE--LAX, cargo cutoff passed. $A_{k_2}^{\text{loc}}$ contains the
        pickup arc, the origin CFS handover, and the CI5232 air arc. LAX-onward arcs
        (destination CFS handover, final delivery) remain open and re-plannable.
  \item $k_3$ --- \texttt{IN\_TRANSIT}: airborne on CV9701 HKG--ANC; onward CV9702
        ANC--JFK already booked with ULDs built at HKG. $A_{k_3}^{\text{loc}}$
        contains all arcs from origin through the CV9702 air arc; only destination
        CFS and final-delivery arcs remain free.
\end{itemize}
The MILP solves over all three shipments together, with $k_2$'s and $k_3$'s
committed arcs fixed; capacity contributed by locked arcs counts against the same
P.2--P.7 capacity caps as $k_1$'s open decisions.

\paragraph{Capacity accounting.}
Locked shipments still consume capacity on their committed arcs. Because constraints
P.2--P.7 sum over $k \in K$ (not $K \setminus K^{\text{loc}}$), and the locked
variables are fixed to their committed values, locked-shipment contributions appear
on the LHS automatically. No special pre-subtraction from RHS is needed.

\paragraph{Cost handling.}
Locked-arc costs (sunk costs) are retained in the objective for full traceability:
the objective value reports total cost across all shipments and arcs. Because locked
variables are constants, sunk-cost terms do not affect the optimizer's argmin --- the
optimizer minimizes over open decisions only, but the reported objective value
includes everything. Per-shipment cost attribution and reporting are therefore
direct, no separate sunk-cost ledger needed.

\paragraph{Supply-side lock invalidation.}
Lock semantics so far capture \emph{demand-side} commitments: cargo dispatched, AWBs
filed, ULDs built, flights departed. Real operations also generate \emph{supply-side}
events that invalidate previously-committed routings without any change on the
shipment's side:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Flight cancellation} --- carrier cancels the entire flight (mechanical,
        weather, crew). Communicated via IATA Cargo-IMP \texttt{FFM}
        amendment or Cargo-XML cancellation message; on-the-ground
        notification via \texttt{FSU} status updates.
  \item \textbf{Equipment swap} --- carrier swaps aircraft (e.g., 777F $\to$ A330F due to
        maintenance). Same flight number, different $\text{ac}(f)$, different
        $U_f$ set, potentially different position counts $P_{f,u}$. The committed ULD
        assignment may no longer fit.
  \item \textbf{Allocation pull / capacity reduction} --- carrier reduces the
        forwarder's contracted allocation on a future flight (rare on hard BSAs;
        more common on soft BSAs near departure).
  \item \textbf{Cargo cutoff shift} --- carrier moves $\text{CGC}_{f,\tau}$ earlier
        on a specific flight due to operational constraints.
\end{itemize}

Define the \emph{supply event set} $\mathcal{E}^{\text{sup}}$ as the time-ordered
sequence of these events. Each event $e \in \mathcal{E}^{\text{sup}}$ carries
$(\text{flight\_id}, \text{event\_type}, \text{payload}, \text{observed\_at})$.

\paragraph{Invalidation rule.}
For each shipment $k \in K^{\text{loc}}$ with locked-on arcs $A_k^{\text{loc}}$, an
event $e$ \emph{invalidates the lock on arc $(i,j)$} when any of:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,label=(\alph*)]
  \item Flight cancellation: $e.\text{event\_type} = \text{\texttt{flight\_cancelled}}$
        and $(i,j)$ corresponds to the cancelled flight.
  \item Equipment swap rendering the locked ULD position infeasible:
        $e.\text{event\_type} = \text{\texttt{equipment\_swap}}$ and the new
        $\text{ac}(f)$ no longer supports the locked
        $(u, \text{position\_count})$ assignment for $(i,j)$.
  \item Allocation pull below committed quantity:
        $e.\text{event\_type} = \text{\texttt{allocation\_pull}}$ and the
        new allocation $<$ the shipment's locked $z_{f,u}^c$ contribution.
  \item Cargo cutoff moved earlier past the cargo's now-feasible ready time:
        $e.\text{event\_type} = \text{\texttt{cgc\_shift}}$ and the new
        $\text{CGC}_{f,\ctype_k}$ violates Eq.~\ref{eq:cutoff-feasibility} given the
        shipment's current state.
\end{enumerate}
On invalidation, the affected arc is removed from $A_k^{\text{loc}}$, the associated
$\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{b}$ are unfrozen, and the shipment is enqueued for a
\emph{rescue re-solve} --- a routing run with the invalidated arc treated as
unavailable and downstream arcs (any locked arcs that depended on the invalidated arc
to be feasible) likewise unlocked.

\paragraph{Rolling-horizon controller responsibilities.}
The supply event stream is consumed by the rolling-horizon controller (not the MILP
itself); the controller is responsible for:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Subscribing to carrier supply events: Cargo-IMP \texttt{FFM} flight-manifest
        amendments and \texttt{FSU} status updates, Cargo-XML flight-cancellation
        messages, and bilateral allocation-update channels per BSA contract.
  \item Applying invalidation rules to active locks and updating shipment state.
  \item Enqueuing rescue re-solves with appropriate priority (departure imminent
        $=$ urgent; days out $=$ normal batch).
  \item Surfacing the disruption to operators with the affected shipment list and the
        triggering event.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{P1 deferred --- proactive supply-side risk modeling.}
MVP reacts to supply events as they arrive. P1 extension: model supply-side
reliability (per-flight cancellation probability, per-carrier equipment-swap
likelihood) and let the optimizer weight options accordingly (e.g., prefer a more
expensive but more reliable routing on critical shipments). Integrates with the
probabilistic-transit-time deferred item.

\paragraph{Booking-confirmation rejection recovery (workflow, not model).}
When the optimizer recommends a routing, the operator (or autonomous agent) initiates
a carrier booking via the operating carrier's API or sales channel. The carrier
responds with a status: \texttt{KK} (confirmed), \texttt{RQ} (requested, awaiting
review), or \texttt{NS} (no space / rejected); operational refusals at the cargo
terminal (cargo overweight per piece, missing screening cert, DGR documentation gap)
also surface here. Rejection is not a model constraint --- the MILP cannot solve a
booking that doesn't exist yet. The workflow:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Rejection event is logged on the shipment record (\texttt{booking\_status\_per\_flight}, \texttt{shipment\_attributes.md} \S3.6).
  \item The rejected flight is added to $A_k^{\text{loc-off}}$ for the shipment, so the
        next solve does not propose it again.
  \item The shipment re-enters the next routing batch (scheduled re-solve) or is
        flagged for an urgent manual run if departure is imminent and the SLA at risk.
  \item No model change required --- existing locked-off arc semantics
        (\S\ref{sec:locked-commitments}) handle the exclusion; existing rolling-horizon
        controller handles the re-queue.
\end{enumerate}
Persistent rejection on the same (carrier, lane) signals a deeper issue (capacity
sold out, contract dispute, embargo not yet in the catalog) which surfaces through the
operator-facing exception queue rather than through silent retry.

\paragraph{Lock-induced infeasibility.}
If the locked prefix is itself infeasible w.r.t.\ downstream constraints (e.g., the
committed first leg arrives at hub too late for any feasible onward, or the committed
prefix already violates the deadline), the MILP becomes infeasible. The
rolling-horizon controller treats this as a structured \emph{rescue} event, not a
generic MILP infeasibility: it is routed to operator review with the specific
violated constraint flagged. Resolution paths --- contract buyout, deadline
re-negotiation with the shipper, partial cancellation --- are operator-driven in
MVP. P1 extension: model lock-breaking with penalty cost (Section~\ref{sec:deferred},
item~\ref{item:lock-buyout}).

\subsection{Service Products and Service-Level Commitments}
\label{sec:service-products}

Forwarders sell named \emph{service products} to shippers --- bundled commitments
covering transit-time, allowed modes, allowed carriers, equipment, and handling
tier. A shipper buys ``Premium Air Express, 3-day door-to-door'' or ``Multimodal
Economy, 25-day''; every routing decision for that shipment must honor the bundle.
This subsection defines the catalog, the per-shipment binding, and the
filter-and-constraint integration with the routing MILP.

\paragraph{Service-product catalog.}
Let $P$ be the per-tenant catalog of service products, indexed by $p$. Each
$p \in P$ is a tuple:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9.5cm}}
\toprule
Field & Description \\
\midrule
$\text{id}(p)$ & Stable product identifier (e.g., \texttt{PRM\_AIR\_EXP}, \texttt{STD\_AIR}, \texttt{MM\_ECON}) \\
$\text{name}(p)$ & Display name shown on contracts and quotes \\
$\text{mode\_allow}(p)$ & Subset of $\{\text{AIR}, \text{OCEAN}, \text{GROUND}\}$. AIR-only restricts $A_k$ to air arcs and incident ground arcs; multimodal allows arc selection across modes. \\
$\text{carrier\_allow}(p)$ & Set of operating-carrier codes that may be used, or \texttt{ANY} \\
$\text{carrier\_deny}(p)$ & Set of operating-carrier codes that may not be used; overrides allow if both present (defensive) \\
$\text{ac\_type\_allow}(p)$ & Set of aircraft types (\texttt{FREIGHTER}, \texttt{PAX\_BELLY}, ...) acceptable for this product, or \texttt{ANY} \\
$T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ & Transit-time SLA: maximum door-to-door duration, in hours \\
$\text{handling\_tier}(p)$ & Priority signal: \texttt{priority} / \texttt{standard} / \texttt{economy}; consumed by the rolling-horizon scheduler, not the within-batch MILP \\
$\text{cargo\_type\_min}(p)$ & Minimum cargo-handling certification required (e.g., \texttt{COLD\_CHAIN}, \texttt{PHARMA}, \texttt{GEN}); composes with the cargo-type compatibility in P.16 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Per-shipment binding.}
Each shipment $k \in K$ carries a foreign key $\text{sp}(k) \in P$ resolving to its
contracted service product. At solve time, the bundle attributes pre-filter the
candidate arc set $A_k$ and contribute one hard constraint (transit-time SLA).
Service products are tenant-scoped --- different tenants offer different catalogs.

\paragraph{Example catalog (illustrative).}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllllr}
\toprule
ID & Mode & Carrier & Aircraft & SLA & Handling \\
\midrule
\texttt{PRM\_AIR\_EXP} & AIR & \{CX, BR, CV, LH, EK\} & FREIGHTER & 72h & priority \\
\texttt{STD\_AIR} & AIR & ANY & ANY & 144h & standard \\
\texttt{ECON\_AIR} & AIR & ANY & ANY & 240h & economy \\
\texttt{MM\_EXPEDITED} & AIR, OCEAN & ANY & ANY & 240h & priority \\
\texttt{MM\_STD} & AIR, OCEAN & ANY & ANY & 600h & standard \\
\texttt{MM\_ECON} & AIR, OCEAN & ANY & ANY & 840h & economy \\
\texttt{OCN\_EXP} & OCEAN & \{MSC, CMA, MAERSK\} & --- & 600h & priority \\
\texttt{OCN\_STD} & OCEAN & ANY & --- & 960h & standard \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Integration with subgraph construction.}
The service-product bundle for shipment $k$ contributes four predicates to the
subgraph pre-filter (Section~\ref{sec:prefilter}, flight reachability pass):
\begin{align}
  \text{mode\_ok}(k, f) \;&\defeq\; \text{AIR} \in \text{mode\_allow}(\text{sp}(k)) \label{eq:sp-mode-ok} \\
  \text{carrier\_ok}(k, f) \;&\defeq\;
    \bigl(\text{carrier\_allow}(\text{sp}(k)) = \texttt{ANY} \;\vee\; \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) \in \text{carrier\_allow}(\text{sp}(k))\bigr) \notag \\
    & \quad \wedge \;\; \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) \notin \text{carrier\_deny}(\text{sp}(k))
    \label{eq:sp-carrier-ok} \\
  \text{ac\_type\_ok}(k, f) \;&\defeq\;
    \text{ac\_type\_allow}(\text{sp}(k)) = \texttt{ANY} \;\vee\; \text{ac}(f) \in \text{ac\_type\_allow}(\text{sp}(k))
    \label{eq:sp-ac-ok}
\end{align}
A flight $f$ is admitted to $A_k$ only if all three predicates hold (in addition to
the existing cutoff, embargo, lithium, cargo-type, and physical-fit checks).
Analogous predicates apply to ocean and ground arcs for the mode filter; carrier and
aircraft-type filters are vacuous on non-air arcs.

\paragraph{Service-level commitment as constraint.}
The transit-time SLA $T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ enters the MILP as a hard constraint (P.20,
Section~\ref{sec:constraints}). For shipments with no operative SLA, set
$T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ to a large value (or $T_k^{\text{dead}}$) so the constraint is
vacuous. The effective bound is $\min\bigl(T_p^{\text{SLA}},\, T_k^{\text{dead}}\bigr)$:
deadline and SLA are independent commitments, both must hold, the tighter binds.

\paragraph{Why service products and not loose flags.}
Operationally, forwarders sell bundled products to shippers and contracts reference
those bundles by name. Modeling commitments as a catalog keeps the constraints
consistent across all shipments under the same contract, surfaces the bundle as a
first-class entity for billing and policy management, and matches how operators
reason about exceptions (``Premium Air Express shipments must never go via X''
rather than ``shipment-37428 must never go via X''). The catalog is mutable by
tenant policy --- new products are added without re-architecting the optimizer.

\paragraph{SLA violation in MVP.}
$T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ is enforced hard in MVP. If the locked prefix or the available
supply cannot meet the SLA, the solve is infeasible and the result is escalated as
a \emph{rescue event} (same channel as deadline misses and lock-induced
infeasibility; Section~\ref{sec:locked-commitments}). The operator chooses among
upgrade-to-premium-air, SLA renegotiation, or paying the OTP penalty out-of-band.
P1 extension: soft SLA with explicit OTP penalty $\pi^{\text{OTP}}_p$, allowing the
MILP to elect the violation when the upgrade cost exceeds the penalty
(Section~\ref{sec:deferred}, item~\ref{item:sla-soft-otp}).

\subsection{Carrier Policy and Rules Resolution}
\label{sec:carrier-policy}

Beyond the service-product bundle, forwarders maintain a layered set of
carrier-level rules: tenant-wide blacklists (e.g., payment disputes, sanctions,
reliability incidents); shipper-lane-specific allow/deny lists (contractual
restrictions per shipper $\times$ lane); lane-level preferences (where contract rate
is favorable); commodity-level rules (DGR-certified carriers for hazmat). These
rules layer \emph{above} the service product and are tenant-mutable in real time
without re-deploying the optimizer.

\paragraph{Rule cascade and hierarchy.}
Rules are organized in a precedence order, evaluated outside the MILP by a separate
\emph{rules-engine component} (cf.\ \texttt{build\_plan.md} component split). The
output of the rules engine is a per-shipment resolved triple. Cascade levels, from
broadest to narrowest:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Tenant carrier blacklist:} forwarder-wide hard deny.
  \item \textbf{Shipper-lane allow/deny:} per (shipper, lane) contractual restriction.
  \item \textbf{Service product bundle:} $\text{carrier\_allow}(\text{sp}(k))$,
        $\text{carrier\_deny}(\text{sp}(k))$ from Section~\ref{sec:service-products}.
  \item \textbf{Lane-level preference:} soft bias toward favorable contract carriers.
  \item \textbf{Commodity-rule overlay:} DGR/cold-chain/pharma-grade carrier restrictions
        derived from cargo type and lithium taxonomy.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Conflict semantics: deny wins.}
When layers conflict, the resolution rule is \emph{deny wins over allow}, regardless
of layer rank. Concretely, let $\mathcal{D}_k = \bigcup_{\ell} \text{deny}_\ell(k)$
(union of all hard denies across layers $\ell$) and let $\mathcal{A}_k =
\bigcap_{\ell : \text{allow}_\ell(k) \neq \texttt{ANY}} \text{allow}_\ell(k)$
(intersection of all explicit allows; \texttt{ANY} contributes the full carrier
universe). The resolved hard-allowed carrier set for shipment $k$ is:
\begin{equation}
  C_k^{\text{allow}} \;\defeq\; \mathcal{A}_k \;\setminus\; \mathcal{D}_k
  \label{eq:carrier-allow}
\end{equation}
This guarantees that an explicit deny at \emph{any} layer is honored. Rationale:
denies represent intentional safety/compliance/contract decisions; silently
overriding them by a higher-tier allow would be a defect. The corresponding
hard-denied set is $C_k^{\text{deny}} = \mathcal{D}_k$ (used as belt-and-suspenders
in the subgraph filter; redundant with $C_k^{\text{allow}}$ but exposed for audit).

\paragraph{Preferred-carrier set $C_k^{\text{pref}}$.}
Soft preferences from any layer (lane-level, shipper-level, commodity-rule) are
unioned without override semantics into a single preferred set:
\begin{equation}
  C_k^{\text{pref}} \;\defeq\; \biggl( \bigcup_{\ell} \text{prefer}_\ell(k) \biggr)
                              \;\cap\; C_k^{\text{allow}}
  \label{eq:carrier-pref}
\end{equation}
The intersection with $C_k^{\text{allow}}$ ensures a preferred carrier that is also
hard-denied does not leak through (deny still wins).

\paragraph{Subgraph integration.}
The service-product predicate $\text{carrier\_ok}(k, f)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:sp-carrier-ok})
is replaced at subgraph construction by the policy-resolved version:
\begin{equation}
  \text{carrier\_ok}(k, f) \;\defeq\;
    \bigl(C_k^{\text{allow}} = \texttt{ANY} \,\vee\, \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) \in C_k^{\text{allow}}\bigr)
    \;\wedge\; \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) \notin C_k^{\text{deny}}
  \label{eq:carrier-ok-resolved}
\end{equation}
The service-product carrier fields feed the cascade (as layer~3); they no longer
gate the subgraph directly. This single change consolidates all carrier-level
filtering through the rules engine.

\paragraph{Operating vs.\ marketing carrier scope.}
Each rule in the cascade names which carrier identity it evaluates against. The
shipment-record fields are operating carrier $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f)$ (the
airline physically operating the aircraft, used by IATA ULD pool / capacity / lithium
acceptance / screening certification) and marketing carrier $\text{carrier}^{\text{mk}}(f)$
(the airline whose code prefixes the AWB; for codeshares these differ).
Each rule's \texttt{scope} JSONB carries a \texttt{carrier\_basis} field:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{op} (default for compliance/regulatory rules): evaluates the rule's
        carrier list against $\text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f)$. Used when the rule
        concerns physical aircraft operation, ULD pool ownership, or operational
        safety profile (e.g., regulatory blacklist after an incident).
  \item \texttt{mk} (default for shipper-contract rules): evaluates against
        $\text{carrier}^{\text{mk}}(f)$. Used when the shipper's master service
        agreement names a carrier on the AWB regardless of underlying operation
        (``no UA on our pharma lane'' typically means UA-prefix AWB, even when
        operated by LH or NH under codeshare).
  \item \texttt{either} (when explicitly set): rule applies if either op or mk
        matches. Conservative interpretation; used when the rule writer was not
        explicit.
\end{itemize}
The resolved $\text{carrier\_ok}(k, f)$ predicate (Eq.~\ref{eq:carrier-ok-resolved})
applies each rule's \texttt{carrier\_basis} to compute $C_k^{\text{allow}}$ and
$C_k^{\text{deny}}$. When tenants migrate from a pre-Task-\#8 carrier-only model,
default values are: tenant blacklist $\to$ \texttt{op}; shipper-lane $\to$
\texttt{mk}; service-product carrier list $\to$ \texttt{op}; lane preference $\to$
\texttt{op}. Tenants override per-rule as their contract language dictates.

\paragraph{Solve strategy: lexicographic two-pass for soft preference.}
Soft preferences enter the optimizer via a lexicographic two-pass solve, not as a
penalty term in the objective. The two passes share all hard constraints
(P.1--P.21).
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Pass 1 --- cost minimization.} Solve the MILP as defined in
        Sections~\ref{sec:constraints}--\ref{sec:objective}; obtain optimal cost $z^*$.
  \item \textbf{Pass 2 --- preference maximization.} Re-solve with the same
        constraints plus a cost-ceiling constraint
        $\text{cost}(\text{solution}) \leq z^* + \varepsilon^{\text{pref}}$, replacing
        the objective with the preferred-carrier count:
        \begin{equation}
          \max \quad \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{f \in F^k :\, \text{carrier}^{\text{op}}(f) \in C_k^{\text{pref}}}
                     \sum_{c \in C_f}\sum_{u \in U_f} y_{f,u,k}^c
          \label{eq:pass2-obj}
        \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The tolerance parameter $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} \geq 0$ (tenant-configurable,
default $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} = 0$ exact or a small percentage of $z^*$ such as
$0.5\%$) controls how much cost the optimizer may concede to honor preferences.
When $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} = 0$, Pass~2 selects the cost-optimal solution that
maximizes preferred-carrier usage among ties.

\paragraph{MIP-gap interaction with $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}}$ (avoid infeasible Pass~2).}
HiGHS returns Pass~1 cost $z^*$ within a relative MIP gap $g$ (typically
$\sim 1\%$ in production; see \S\ref{sec:deferred} solver-strategy notes). The true
optimum lies in $[z^*(1 - g),\, z^*]$. Setting $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} = 0$ then
imposes a Pass-2 cost ceiling at the \emph{optimistic} lower bound of that interval,
which can render Pass~2 infeasible. Required: $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} \geq g \cdot z^*$
(or equivalently, solve Pass~1 to a 0\% MIP gap, which is only practical at small
scale). Recommended default: $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} = \max(0.5\% \cdot z^*,\,
g \cdot z^*)$. This is a standard $\varepsilon$-constraint-method gotcha (Haimes 1971;
Ehrgott, \emph{Multicriteria Optimization} 2005, \S4).

\paragraph{Why lexicographic and not penalty cost.}
A penalty-cost term in the objective conflates two different things (dollars and
preference) under a single coefficient that has no natural calibration --- if the
penalty is too low, preferences are ignored; too high, preferences silently
dominate. Lexicographic two-pass keeps the objectives orthogonal: cost is bounded
first, preference fills the remaining freedom. The cost ceiling
$z^* + \varepsilon^{\text{pref}}$ is an explicit tenant-policy choice (``we will
spend up to \$X more to honor preferences''), which is interpretable and auditable.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
Tenant \texttt{ACME\_FWD}. Shipment $k$ from shipper \texttt{Beta Corp} on lane
TPE$\to$JFK, service product \texttt{PRM\_AIR\_EXP} ($\text{carrier\_allow} =
\{\text{CX, BR, CV, LH, EK, KE}\}$).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{4cm}l}
\toprule
Layer & Rule contribution & Running state \\
\midrule
Tenant blacklist & deny \{KE\} & allow $=$ \{CX, BR, CV, LH, EK\} \\
Shipper-lane (\texttt{Beta Corp}, TPE$\to$JFK) & prefer \{CX, BR\}; deny \{AA\} & AA already excluded; pref $=$ \{CX, BR\} \\
Service product \texttt{PRM\_AIR\_EXP} & allow \{CX, BR, CV, LH, EK, KE\} & layer~3 intersection no change \\
Lane TPE$\to$JFK & prefer \{CV\} (contract rate) & pref $=$ \{CX, BR, CV\} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Resolved: $C_k^{\text{allow}} = \{\text{CX, BR, CV, LH, EK}\}$,
$C_k^{\text{deny}} = \{\text{KE, AA}\}$, $C_k^{\text{pref}} = \{\text{CX, BR, CV}\}$.

With $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}} = 0.5\%$: Pass~1 returns cost-optimal solution with
some allocation across non-preferred (LH, EK) carriers. Pass~2 re-solves with
$\text{cost} \leq z^*(1.005)$; if reshuffling to preferred carriers fits within the
ceiling, Pass~2 returns a solution preferring CX/BR/CV. If not, Pass~2 returns the
same solution as Pass~1.

\paragraph{Where the rules engine lives.}
The rules engine is a separate component (\texttt{build\_plan.md} components
inventory). It exposes a single function call:
\begin{center}
\texttt{resolve\_carrier\_policy(tenant\_id, shipment) $\to$ $(C^{\text{allow}}, C^{\text{deny}}, C^{\text{pref}})$}
\end{center}
The routing MILP consumes the resolved triple per shipment at solve time. The rules
engine itself is described in a separate LaTeX model (\texttt{rules\_engine.tex}, to
be drafted in Phase~1).

\paragraph{Storage and versioning.}
Carrier-policy rules, snapshots, and per-run bindings live in the generic
\texttt{policy\_rules} / \texttt{policy\_snapshots} /
\texttt{routing\_run\_policy\_bindings} schema (\texttt{data\_model.md} \S4). The
same generic framework backs embargo, lithium, ULD-interchange, and
service-product policies. Every solve binds to a snapshot at run start; rule edits
between snapshot creation and solve completion do not affect the in-flight solve.
Emergency removal $=$ soft-delete (\texttt{status = soft\_deleted}); rows are
preserved for audit. The reproducibility query in \texttt{data\_model.md} \S4.4
reconstructs the exact rule state of any past run.

\paragraph{Out of MVP scope (deferred).}
Time-windowed rules (peak-season vs.\ off-peak), conditional rules (``prefer
freighter when commodity is electronics''), and machine-learned preference weights
from override history are deferred to the rules engine model and to Phase~5
constraint learning.

%%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Commodity-Specific Subgraph Construction}
\label{sec:prefilter}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

Before the MILP is built, we construct $G(N_k, A_k)$ for each commodity $k$. The subgraph
contains only arcs that are time-feasible, cargo-type-compatible, and reachable on a complete
$o(k) \to d(k)$ path.

\paragraph{Construction algorithm.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pickup pass.} For each origin CFS $j \in N_{\text{CFS,O}}$, compute earliest
        CFS arrival:
        \[
          \tau_k(j) \defeq t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} + \mu_{o(k),j}^{\text{PU}}
        \]
        Arc $(o(k), j) \in A_{\text{PU}}$ is candidate iff there exists a downstream feasible path.

  \item \textbf{Origin CFS pass.} For each CFS-to-airport arc $(j, a) \in A_{\text{OC}}$ where
        $j \in N_{\text{CFS,O}}$ and $a \in N_{\text{AP,O}}$, compute earliest airport arrival:
        \[
          \tau_k(a) \defeq \tau_k(j) + \mu_{j,a}^{\text{OC}} + \beta^{\text{O}}
        \]

  \item \textbf{Flight reachability pass.} For each flight $f \in F$ (arc $(a, b) \in A_{\text{AIR}}$):
        $f$ is candidate for $k$ iff
        \begin{align*}
          &\tau_k(a) + \text{prep\_time}_k \leq \text{CO}_{f,k}^* \quad &\text{(meets most restrictive cutoff)}\\
          &\text{ETD}_f + \mu_f^{\text{AIR}} + \min_{(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{DC}}}\!\tilde{\mu}^{\text{DC}}_{ij,k} + \min_{(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{FD}}}\!\tilde{\mu}^{\text{FD}}_{ij,k} \leq T_k^{\text{dead}} \quad &\text{(deadline reachable)}\\
          &\text{ETD}_f + \mu_f^{\text{AIR}} + \min_{(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{DC}}}\!\tilde{\mu}^{\text{DC}}_{ij,k} + \min_{(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{FD}}}\!\tilde{\mu}^{\text{FD}}_{ij,k} \leq t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} + T_p^{\text{SLA}} \quad &\text{(SLA reachable)}\\
          &\text{embargo\_ok}(k, f) = \text{True} \quad &\text{(no active embargo matches)}\\
          &\text{lithium\_ok}(k, f) = \text{True} \quad &\text{(lithium acceptance + SOC + non-DDR)}\\
          &\text{screening\_ok}(k, f) = \text{True} \quad &\text{(TSA CCSF / EU ACC3-RA3 if required)}\\
          &\text{mode\_ok}(k, f) \,\wedge\, \text{carrier\_ok}(k, f) \,\wedge\, \text{ac\_type\_ok}(k, f) \quad &\text{(service-product compliance)}\\
          &\text{cargo\_type\_ok}(k, f) = \text{True} \quad &\text{(cargo-type compatibility, Eq.~\ref{eq:cargo-type-ok})}\\
          &\exists\, u \in U_f : D_k \text{ fits } u  \quad &\text{(physical fit in some ULD)}
        \end{align*}
        where \(\text{CO}_{f,k}^*\) is the shipment-indexed effective cutoff (Eq.~\ref{eq:effective-cutoff}),
        \(\text{embargo\_ok}(k, f)\) is the embargo-feasibility predicate
        (Eq.~\ref{eq:embargo-feasibility}), \(\text{lithium\_ok}(k, f)\) is the
        lithium feasibility predicate (Eq.~\ref{eq:lithium-ok}), and
        $\text{mode\_ok}$, $\text{carrier\_ok}$, $\text{ac\_type\_ok}$ are the
        service-product predicates (Eqs.~\ref{eq:sp-mode-ok}--\ref{eq:sp-ac-ok}), and
        $\text{screening\_ok}$ is the cargo-screening certification predicate
        (Eq.~\ref{eq:screening-ok}). For non-lithium shipments, \(\text{lithium\_ok}\)
        is trivially \(\text{True}\); for freighter flights or non-regulated
        destinations, $\text{screening\_ok}$ is trivially \(\text{True}\).

  \item \textbf{Hub transitions pass.} For each pair of consecutive air arcs $(f, g)$ at hub $h$:
        compute the \emph{optimistic} arrival time $\tau_k(g_{\text{start}}) = \text{ETA}_f
        + \min_{u \in U_f \cap U_g} \text{MCT}^*_{f,g,u}$, where $\text{MCT}^*_{f,g,u}$ is
        defined in (Eq.~\ref{eq:effective-mct}). Optimistic because at subgraph time the
        ULD type to be used by $k$ is not yet known; pre-filter is conservative on arc
        admission. P.14 will enforce the exact MCT at solve time based on the actual
        ULD type selected.

  \item \textbf{Destination CFS \& delivery pass.} For each destination airport $b \in N_{\text{AP,D}}$
        reachable: include arc $(b, c) \in A_{\text{DC}}$ where $c \in N_{\text{CFS,D}}$, then
        $(c, d(k)) \in A_{\text{FD}}$.

  \item \textbf{Reachability sweep.} Forward BFS from $o(k)$ and backward BFS from $d(k)$.
        Retain only arcs on paths reachable from both ends.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Flight-indexed shorthand.}
Several constraints and objective terms reference flights rather than arcs. For
notational compactness, we write:
\begin{equation}
  x_f^k \;\defeq\; x_{ij}^k \quad \text{where } (i,j) \text{ is the air arc of flight } f
                            \text{ (i.e., } f(i,j) = f \text{)}
  \label{eq:x-flight-shorthand}
\end{equation}
This is purely a shorthand; $x_f^k$ is not a separate decision variable. Every
appearance of $x_f^k$ in P.14, the McCormick linearizations of $\xi$ and $\chi$
(\S\ref{sec:linearization}), and the objective is mechanically equivalent to
$x_{ij}^k$ with $(i,j)$ the unique air arc of $f$.

\section{Constraints}
\label{sec:constraints}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection*{P.1 — Flow Conservation}

For each commodity $k$ and each node $n \in N_k$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j : (n,j) \in A^k} x_{nj}^k \;-\; \sum_{i : (i,n) \in A^k} x_{in}^k
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    +1 & \text{if } n = o(k) \\
    -1 & \text{if } n = d(k) \\
    0  & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:P1}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{P.2 — ULD Volume Capacity (per flight, per ULD type, per contract)}

For each flight $f \in F$, each ULD type $u \in U_f$, each contract $c \in C_f$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k \in K} v_k \cdot y_{f,u,k}^c \;\leq\; \eta \cdot V_u \cdot z_{f,u}^c
  \label{eq:P2}
\end{equation}
Total volume loaded into contract-$c$ ULDs of type $u$ on flight $f$ cannot exceed the fill-rate-capped
capacity of the claimed ULDs.

\subsection*{P.3 — ULD Weight Capacity}

For each $f, u, c$ as above:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k \in K} cw_k \cdot y_{f,u,k}^c \;\leq\; W_u \cdot z_{f,u}^c
  \label{eq:P3}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{P.4 — Per-Flight Contract Allocation Cap}

For each flight $f \in F$, ULD type $u$, contract $c \in C_f$:
\begin{equation}
  z_{f,u}^c \;\leq\; \text{alloc}(c, f, u)
  \label{eq:P4}
\end{equation}
Forwarder cannot claim more ULDs of type $u$ on flight $f$ than the contract allocates.

\subsection*{P.5 — Aircraft Physical Position Cap (across all contracts)}

For each flight $f \in F$, ULD type $u \in U_f$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{c \in C_f} z_{f,u}^c \;\leq\; P_{f,u}
  \label{eq:P5}
\end{equation}
Total ULDs of type $u$ across all contracts cannot exceed the aircraft's physical positions.

\subsection*{P.6 — Period Contract Allocation Cap}

For each contract $c$, ULD type $u$, period $t$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{f \in F_c(t)} z_{f,u}^c \;\leq\; \text{rem}(c, u, t)
  \label{eq:P6}
\end{equation}
Cumulative ULDs claimed in period $t$ under contract $c$ cannot exceed the period's remaining
allocation (after prior bookings).

\subsection*{P.7 — Period Minimum Utilization (hard BSA take-or-pay)}

For each contract $c$ with $\text{hard}_c = 1$, ULD type $u$, period $t$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{f \in F_c(t)} z_{f,u}^c \;\geq\; \underline{M}_{c,u,t}
  \label{eq:P7}
\end{equation}
$\underline{M}_{c,u,t}$ is the per-run minimum allocation passed in from the upstream
period-commitment model. For soft BSAs, $\underline{M}_{c,u,t} = 0$.

\subsection*{P.8 — Arc-to-ULD Linkage}

For each shipment $k$, flight $f$ with $(i,j) = f^{-1}$ in $A^k_{\text{AIR}}$:
\begin{equation}
  x_{ij}^k \;=\; \sum_{c \in C_f} \sum_{u \in U_f} y_{f,u,k}^c \;+\; b_{f,k}
  \label{eq:P8}
\end{equation}
If shipment $k$ uses arc $(i,j)$, it is loaded via exactly one contract ULD or the spot rate.

\subsection*{P.9 — Spot vs.\ Contract Exclusivity (per shipment per flight)}

Implicit in P.8: at most one of $\{y_{f,u,k}^c\}_{c,u} \cup \{b_{f,k}\}$ equals 1 when $x_{ij}^k = 1$.
Formally:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{c \in C_f} \sum_{u \in U_f} y_{f,u,k}^c \;+\; b_{f,k} \;\leq\; 1
  \label{eq:P9}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{P.10 — Effective Chargeable Weight (pivot weight linearization)}

For each contract $c$, flight $f$, ULD type $u$ — linearizes $\max(\text{actual}, \pi \cdot z)$:
\begin{align}
  C_{f,u,c} \;&\geq\; \sum_{k \in K} cw_k \cdot y_{f,u,k}^c &&\text{(at least the actual loaded weight)} \label{eq:P10a} \\
  C_{f,u,c} \;&\geq\; \pi_{c,u} \cdot z_{f,u}^c &&\text{(at least the pivot floor)} \label{eq:P10b}
\end{align}
Since $C_{f,u,c}$ is minimized in the objective (it multiplies the rate $r_c$), at optimum
$C_{f,u,c} = \max(\sum_k cw_k \cdot y, \pi_{c,u} \cdot z)$ as required. See Section~\ref{sec:linearization} for the linearization argument.

\subsection*{P.11 — Effective Cutoff (Cargo + Documents + Customs Filing)}

For each shipment $k$ and each air arc $(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{AIR}}$ with $f = f(i,j)$:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(i) + \text{prep\_time}_k \;\leq\; \text{CO}_{f,k}^* + M^{P11}_{f,k}(1 - x_{ij}^k)
  \label{eq:P11}
\end{equation}
where $\text{CO}_{f,k}^*$ is the shipment-indexed effective cutoff
(Eq.~\ref{eq:effective-cutoff}) and $M^{P11}_{f,k}$ is the per-(f,k) tight big-M
defined in \S\ref{sec:bigm-tightening}. The single inequality enforces
the cargo-type-specific cargo cutoff $\text{CGC}_{f,\ctype_k}$, document cutoff
$\text{DCO}_f$, and applicable customs pre-load filing deadlines simultaneously by
binding against the most restrictive in the set.

\subsection*{P.12 — Arrival Time Propagation (ground arcs)}

For each shipment $k$ and each ground arc $(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{PU}} \cup A^k_{\text{OC}} \cup A^k_{\text{DC}} \cup A^k_{\text{FD}}$:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(j) \;\geq\; t_k(i) + \tilde{\mu}_{ij,k}^{\text{G}} - M^{P12}_{ij,k}(1 - x_{ij}^k)
  \label{eq:P12}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mu}_{ij,k}^{\text{G}}$ is the effective transit time on the ground arc
including all applicable dwells, defined per arc type in
Eqs.~\ref{eq:eff-transit-pu}--\ref{eq:eff-transit-fd}
(\S\ref{sec:ground-arc-params}). Origin-side arcs ($A_{\text{PU}}, A_{\text{OC}}$) carry
no shipment-specific dwell; destination-side arcs ($A_{\text{DC}}, A_{\text{FD}}$) carry
$\delta^{\text{cust}}_k$ and $\delta^{\text{rel}}_k$ respectively, both resolved at
intake from shipment attributes. The big-M $M^{P12}_{ij,k}$ is the per-arc tight value
$\tilde{\mu}_{ij,k}^{\text{G}} + \epsilon$ (\S\ref{sec:bigm-tightening}).

\subsection*{P.13 — Arrival Time Propagation (air arcs)}

For each shipment $k$ and each air arc $(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{AIR}}$ with $f = f(i,j)$:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(j) \;\geq\; \text{ETA}_f - M^{P13}_f(1 - x_{ij}^k)
  \label{eq:P13}
\end{equation}
where $M^{P13}_f \defeq \text{ETA}_f + \epsilon$ is the tight big-M for the air-arc
propagation (\S\ref{sec:bigm-tightening}).

\subsection*{P.14 — Hub Minimum Connect Time}

For each shipment $k$ and each pair of consecutive air arcs $(f, g)$ at hub $h$, the
minimum dwell depends on the ULD type used by $k$ on both legs. Define the per-tuple
effective MCT (a constant since $\psi$ is a parameter):
\begin{equation}
  \text{MCT}^*_{f,g,u}
  \;\defeq\; \psi_{f,g,u}\,\text{MCT}^{\text{thru}}_{f,g}
            \;+\; (1 - \psi_{f,g,u})\,\text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g}
  \qquad \forall\, u \in U_f \cap U_g
  \label{eq:effective-mct}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Case 1 --- same ULD type on both arcs.}
For each $u \in U_f \cap U_g$ and each $(k, f, g)$:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(g_{\text{start}}) \;\geq\; \text{ETA}_f \;+\; \text{MCT}^*_{f,g,u}
  \;-\; M^{P14a}_{f,g}\!\biggl( 2 - \sum_{c} y^{c}_{f,u,k} - \sum_{c'} y^{c'}_{g,u,k} \biggr)
  \label{eq:P14a}
\end{equation}
The big-M $M^{P14a}_{f,g}$ deactivates the constraint unless shipment $k$ uses ULD type $u$ on
\emph{both} arcs ($\sum_c y^c_{f,u,k} = 1$ and $\sum_{c'} y^{c'}_{g,u,k} = 1$). Tight
value: $M^{P14a}_{f,g} \defeq \text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} / 2 + \epsilon$
(\S\ref{sec:bigm-tightening}; the divisor 2 reflects the two-indicator deactivator
form here).

\paragraph{Case 2 --- cross-ULD (different type on $f$ vs.\ $g$).}
When $k$ uses different ULD types on $f$ and $g$ (e.g., LD3 on a belly leg, PMC on a
freighter leg), breakdown is operationally forced and $\text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g}$
binds:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(g_{\text{start}}) \;\geq\; \text{ETA}_f \;+\; \text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g}
  \;-\; M^{P14b}_{f,g}\bigl( 1 - \chi_{f,g,k} \bigr)
  \label{eq:P14b}
\end{equation}
Tight value: $M^{P14b}_{f,g} \defeq \text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} + \epsilon$
(\S\ref{sec:bigm-tightening}; single-indicator $(1-\chi)$ form gives a tight $M$
equal to the MCT itself).
where $\chi_{f,g,k}$ is the cross-ULD indicator from \S\ref{sec:linearization}
(defined as $\xi_{f,g,k} - \sum_u \zeta_{f,g,u,k}$). The deactivator
$(1 - \chi_{f,g,k}) = 0$ iff $\chi_{f,g,k} = 1$, i.e., iff $k$ uses both arcs with
different ULD types --- which is exactly when the constraint should bind. The
deactivator $= 1$ when either (i) $k$ does not use both arcs ($\xi = 0$), or (ii) $k$
uses the same ULD type on both ($\sum_u \zeta = 1$, $\chi = 0$; Case~1 governs
instead). This is algebraically tighter than the earlier $(2 - x_f^k - x_g^k + \sum_u
\zeta_{f,g,u,k})$ formulation while reusing the existing $\chi$ definition.

\paragraph{Joint behavior.}
$\zeta$ and Case~2's deactivation indicator are mutually exclusive within a given
$(k, f, g)$ tuple; their sum equals $x_f^k \cdot x_g^k$. Exactly one of (P.14a, P.14b)
binds when both arcs are used, and neither binds otherwise.

\subsection*{P.15 — Deadline}

For each shipment $k$:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(d(k)) \;\leq\; T_k^{\text{dead}}
  \label{eq:P15}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{P.16 — Cargo Type Compatibility}

Define the cargo-type-acceptance predicate per (shipment, flight):
\begin{equation}
  \text{cargo\_type\_ok}(k, f) \;\defeq\;
  \begin{cases}
    \text{True} & \ctype_k = \text{GEN} \\
    \text{dgr}(f) = 1 & \ctype_k = \text{DGR} \\
    \text{per}(f) = 1 & \ctype_k = \text{PER} \\
    \text{val\_capable}(f) = 1 & \ctype_k = \text{VAL} \\
    \text{avi\_capable}(f) = 1 & \ctype_k = \text{AVI} \\
    \text{hum\_capable}(f) = 1 & \ctype_k = \text{HUM}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cargo-type-ok}
\end{equation}
where $\text{dgr}(f)$, $\text{per}(f)$ are defined in \S\ref{sec:flight-params}; the
other three capability flags follow the same per-flight-boolean pattern (introduced
implicitly in the carrier's published cargo acceptance schedule; surfaced in the
flight-parameter table for shipments where the corresponding $\ctype_k$ value
appears).

For each shipment $k$ and each air arc $(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{AIR}}$ with $f = f(i,j)$:
\begin{equation}
  x_{ij}^k \;\leq\; \mathbb{1}\bigl[\text{cargo\_type\_ok}(k, f)\bigr]
  \label{eq:P16}
\end{equation}
The predicate is evaluated at subgraph construction (Section~\ref{sec:prefilter},
step 3); P.16 acts as a defense-in-depth constraint in case a non-compliant arc
slips through pre-filtering.

\subsection*{P.17 — ULD Physical Fit}

For each shipment $k$ and each $(f, u, c)$ combination where $D_k$ does not fit ULD type $u$:
\begin{equation}
  y_{f,u,k}^c \;=\; 0
  \label{eq:P17}
\end{equation}
This is a pre-processing exclusion, not a runtime constraint — fit is determined from
$D_k$ vs.\ ULD contour at subgraph construction. Bin-packing of multiple shipments inside
a single ULD is approximated by the volume constraint P.2; full 3D bin-packing is deferred.

\subsection*{P.18 — Budget Cap (optional, per-shipment-additive components only)}

For each shipment $k$ with $B_k < \infty$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{cost}_k \;\leq\; B_k
  \label{eq:P18}
\end{equation}
where $\text{cost}_k$ is the sum of \emph{per-shipment-additive} cost components only:
\begin{equation}
  \text{cost}_k \;\defeq\;
    \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j) \in A_k} c_{ij}^{\text{G}} \cdot x_{ij}^k}_{\text{ground}}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{\sum_{f \in F^k} \text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f) \cdot b_{f,k}}_{\text{spot}}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{\sum_{(i,j) \in A_k} \text{surcharge\_cost}(k, i, j) \cdot x_{ij}^k}_{\text{Path-A surcharges}}
  \label{eq:cost-k-def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What is \emph{excluded} from $\text{cost}_k$ for budget-cap purposes.}
The joint contracted-ULD cost term $r_c \cdot C_{f,u,c}$ couples multiple shipments
(via the pivot-floor mechanism) and cannot be cleanly attributed to a single shipment
without inventing an allocation rule (proportional to $cw_k$? volume? chargeable
weight?). Different allocation choices produce different P.18 binding behavior.
For MVP, P.18 governs only the per-shipment-additive components above. Rehandling
costs are also excluded (per-(f,g) coupling). The flight-level
$\text{flight\_uld\_surcharge\_cost}(f)$ (Path-B surcharges) is excluded for the
same reason. Operators who need budget caps on the joint-cost contribution should
use a tenant-level cost ceiling on the routing run total, not P.18.

P1 extension: ULD-share allocation rule (e.g., proportional to $cw_k$ relative to
total flight chargeable weight) to include the joint cost in $\text{cost}_k$;
deferred until tenants report concrete need.

\subsection*{P.19 — Locked Commitments}

For each $k \in K^{\text{loc}}$, the decision variables on the locked prefix are pinned
to their committed/observed values via two-sided inequalities (rather than equality
fixes) to give HiGHS presolve clean conflict diagnostics
(\S\ref{sec:locked-commitments}):
\begin{align}
  x_{ij}^k \;&\geq\; \bar{x}_{ij}^k,\quad x_{ij}^k \;\leq\; \bar{x}_{ij}^k
                                    & & \forall\, (i,j) \in A_k^{\text{loc}} \cup A_k^{\text{loc-off}} \\
  y_{f,u,k}^c \;&\geq\; \bar{y}_{f,u,k}^c,\quad y_{f,u,k}^c \;\leq\; \bar{y}_{f,u,k}^c
                                    & & \forall\, (f,u,c) \text{ committed on a locked air arc} \\
  b_{f,k} \;&\geq\; \bar{b}_{f,k},\quad b_{f,k} \;\leq\; \bar{b}_{f,k}
                                    & & \forall\, f \text{ with a committed spot booking} \\
  t_k(i) \;&\geq\; \bar{t}_k(i),\quad t_k(i) \;\leq\; \bar{t}_k(i)
                                    & & \forall\, i \in N \text{ with an observed milestone event}
  \label{eq:P19}
\end{align}
The inequality pair is solver-equivalent to the equality form $x = \bar{x}$, but
HiGHS (and other MIP presolves) produce more interpretable conflict reasons when an
inequality binds in an infeasible direction than when an equality conflicts silently.
For $k \notin K^{\text{loc}}$, no P.19 constraints are generated (full decision space
preserved). Per-arc variable pinning means the optimizer's effective decision space
shrinks only on the locked prefix; capacity contributions from pinned variables flow
through P.2--P.7 automatically because those constraints sum over all $k \in K$.

\paragraph{Pre-solve lock-feasibility check (required, not optional).}
Before the MILP is built, a separate pass evaluates the locked prefix against the
non-lock constraints (P.2--P.7 capacity caps, P.11 cutoff with current state, P.15
deadline, P.20 SLA). If any lock conflicts --- e.g., a committed $\bar{y}$ exceeds
allocation after an upstream allocation pull, or an observed $\bar{t}_k(i)$ is below
the propagated lower bound from earlier locks --- the rolling-horizon controller
surfaces a structured \emph{rescue event} naming the conflicting lock and constraint,
\emph{without attempting the MILP solve}. This produces actionable operator output
(``lock on flight CX880 conflicts with allocation pull; remediate'') instead of a
bare HiGHS \texttt{INFEASIBLE} return. The pre-solve check is part of the
rolling-horizon controller, not the MILP itself; see \S\ref{sec:locked-commitments}
(``Lock-induced infeasibility'') for the workflow.

\subsection*{P.20 — Transit-Time SLA}

For each shipment $k$, let $p = \text{sp}(k) \in P$ be the bound service product
(Section~\ref{sec:service-products}) and $T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ its transit-time SLA:
\begin{equation}
  t_k(d(k)) \;-\; t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} \;\leq\; T_p^{\text{SLA}}
  \label{eq:P20}
\end{equation}
$t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$ is the cargo-ready time (origin door); the LHS is door-to-door
transit duration. The constraint binds when $T_p^{\text{SLA}} < T_k^{\text{dead}} - t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$;
otherwise the deadline (P.15) is the tighter bound and P.20 is vacuous. Both must
hold: the effective deadline for door-to-door transit is
$\min\bigl(T_p^{\text{SLA}},\, T_k^{\text{dead}} - t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}\bigr)$.
Shipments with no operative SLA carry a service product with $T_p^{\text{SLA}} = \infty$
(or any value $\geq T_k^{\text{dead}}$), making P.20 vacuous.

P.20 is enforced hard in MVP. Infeasibility from an unmeetable SLA is surfaced as a
rescue event (Section~\ref{sec:service-products}, ``SLA violation in MVP''),
not as a generic MILP infeasibility. P1 extension: soft SLA with OTP penalty,
Section~\ref{sec:deferred}, item~\ref{item:sla-soft-otp}.

\subsection*{P.21 — Domain and Initial Conditions}

Variable types:
\begin{equation}
  x_{ij}^k, y_{f,u,k}^c, b_{f,k} \in \{0,1\}, \quad
  z_{f,u}^c \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}, \quad
  C_{f,u,c}, t_k(i) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
  \label{eq:P21-domain}
\end{equation}
McCormick auxiliaries from Section~\ref{sec:linearization}: $\zeta_{f,g,u,k}, \xi_{f,g,k} \in \{0,1\}$;
$\chi_{f,g,k} \in [0,1]$ (continuous --- see \S\ref{sec:linearization}, ``Cross-ULD indicator'').

\paragraph{Initial-condition bound on pickup time (cargo-ready window).}
For each shipment $k$, the actual pickup time at the origin door is constrained to
the shipper-offered window:
\begin{equation}
  t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} \;\leq\; t_k(o(k)) \;\leq\; t_k^{\text{rdy,late}}
  \label{eq:P21-pickup-window}
\end{equation}
This enforces the cargo-ready window introduced in \S\ref{sec:ground-arc-params} (Eq.~\ref{eq:cargo-ready-window})
as a hard MILP constraint --- without it, $t_k(o(k))$ would be free in $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$
and the propagation in P.12 would not anchor against the shipper's window. The bounds
are both linear in $t_k(o(k))$ and trivial to add. P.20 (SLA) is anchored against the
parameter $t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$, not against this decision variable, per the
operational design (\S\ref{sec:ground-arc-params}, ``Cargo ready window'').

%%--------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Objective Function}
\label{sec:objective}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

Minimize total cost across all shipments:

\begin{align}
  \min \quad
  & \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{(i,j) \in A^k_{\text{PU}} \cup A^k_{\text{OC}} \cup A^k_{\text{DC}} \cup A^k_{\text{FD}}} c_{ij}^{\text{G}} \cdot x_{ij}^k
    \tag{ground cost} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{c \in C} \sum_{f \in F_c} \sum_{u \in U_f} r_c \cdot C_{f,u,c}
    \tag{contracted ULD cost incl.\ pivot floor} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{f \in F^k} \text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f) \cdot b_{f,k}
    \tag{spot rate cost} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{(i,j) \in A_k} \text{surcharge\_cost}(k, i, j) \cdot x_{ij}^k
    \tag{surcharges --- per-shipment-per-arc, catalog-resolved; \S\ref{sec:surcharge} Path~A} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{f \in F} \text{flight\_uld\_surcharge\_cost}(f)
    \tag{surcharges --- flight-level \texttt{per\_uld} bases; \S\ref{sec:surcharge} Path~B, Eq.~\ref{eq:flight-uld-surcharge}} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{(f,g) \in \text{Hub}(k)} \sum_{u \in U_f \cap U_g} \rho_{f,g,u}^{\text{reULD}} \cdot (1 - \psi_{f,g,u}) \cdot \zeta_{f,g,u,k}
    \tag{rehandling cost --- same-ULD case} \\
  +\;
  & \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{(f,g) \in \text{Hub}(k)} \bar{\rho}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} \cdot \chi_{f,g,k}
    \tag{rehandling cost --- cross-ULD case}
\end{align}

The same-ULD term uses $\zeta_{f,g,u,k}$ (defined in Section~\ref{sec:linearization}):
the indicator that shipment $k$ uses ULD type $u$ on \emph{both} $f$ and $g$. The term
contributes only when $\psi_{f,g,u} = 0$ --- i.e., same ULD type on both legs but no
through-ULD eligibility (e.g., interline pair outside any ULD interchange agreement).

The cross-ULD term uses $\chi_{f,g,k} = \xi_{f,g,k} - \sum_{u \in U_f \cap U_g} \zeta_{f,g,u,k}$
(linearized form defined in Section~\ref{sec:linearization}; the term $\xi_{f,g,k}$
is the McCormick linearization of the product $x_f^k \cdot x_g^k$, also defined
there): the indicator that $k$ uses both arcs but different ULD types.
$\bar{\rho}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g}$ is the cross-type rehandling cost --- typically
equal to or slightly higher than the same-type cost, since cargo must be re-stuffed
into a new ULD shape.

The two cases together cover all re-ULDing situations exactly: same-u with $\psi=0$, and
cross-u (always forced).

\paragraph{Indicator $\mathbb{1}[k \text{ on } f]$.}
Equals $1$ if shipment $k$ uses flight $f$; can be substituted as $\sum_{c,u} y_{f,u,k}^c + b_{f,k}$.

\section{Linearization Details}
\label{sec:linearization}

\subsection{Pivot Weight Linearization (P.10)}

The native cost expression per (contract, flight, ULD type) is:
\[
  \text{cost}_{f,u,c} = r_c \cdot \max\!\Bigl(\sum_k cw_k \cdot y_{f,u,k}^c,\ \pi_{c,u} \cdot z_{f,u}^c\Bigr)
\]
The $\max$ operator is non-linear. Introduce auxiliary variable $C_{f,u,c} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$
with two linear inequality constraints (P.10a, P.10b). Replace the cost term with $r_c \cdot C_{f,u,c}$
in the objective. \textbf{Why this works:} the objective minimizes the weighted sum that includes
$r_c \cdot C_{f,u,c}$, so the optimizer pushes $C_{f,u,c}$ down to the largest binding
constraint, i.e., the maximum of the two right-hand sides. Adds one continuous variable and
two inequality constraints per (contract, flight, ULD type) triple.

\paragraph{P1 alternative --- disaggregated formulation (deferred).}
The current formulation aggregates $z_{f,u}^c$ as a count; $C_{f,u,c}$ is a single
continuous auxiliary per triple. A disaggregated version --- $z_{f,u}^c = \sum_n z^c_{f,u,n}$
where $n$ indexes individual ULD slots, each $z^c_{f,u,n} \in \{0,1\}$, with a
per-slot chargeable-weight auxiliary $C^c_{f,u,n}$ and two-sided inequalities ---
yields a tighter LP relaxation at the cost of $O(\text{alloc}(c, f, u))$ extra
binaries per (c, f, u). Williams \emph{Model Building} \S10.2 establishes the
disaggregation-tightens-LP-relaxation result. This refinement is only worth the
variable-count cost if P.10 turns out to be a binding LP-relaxation ridge in
production; empirical experience in MCNF routing suggests P.7 (period commitments)
and P.14 (hub MCT) bind harder than P.10 in typical air-freight instances. Defer
until LP-gap-source instrumentation post-deployment indicates P.10 dominates.

\subsection{Bilinear Rehandling Cost Linearization}
\label{sec:lin-rehandling}

The hub-pair indicators required by the objective (rehandling cost) and by P.14 are
defined here. The previous draft wrote the rehandling product as
$y_{f,u,k}^c \cdot y_{g,u,k}^{c'}$ with implicit contract indices $c, c'$; the
cleaner formulation aggregates over contracts, since a shipment uses at most one
contract per (flight, ULD type) per (P.9 spot/contract exclusivity).

\paragraph{Same-ULD indicator $\zeta_{f,g,u,k}$ (for $u \in U_f \cap U_g$ only).}
Defined only for ULD types compatible with \emph{both} flights at the hub
($u \in U_f \cap U_g$); for $u$ outside the intersection, the relevant $y$
variables don't exist and $\zeta$ would collapse trivially to 0 --- so we restrict
the definition. Binary variable: $\zeta = 1$ iff $k$ uses ULD type $u$ on
\emph{both} arcs $f$ and $g$, across any contract on each:
\begin{align}
  \zeta_{f,g,u,k} \;&\leq\; \sum_{c} y_{f,u,k}^{c} & & \forall\, u \in U_f \cap U_g \\
  \zeta_{f,g,u,k} \;&\leq\; \sum_{c'} y_{g,u,k}^{c'} & & \forall\, u \in U_f \cap U_g \\
  \zeta_{f,g,u,k} \;&\geq\; \sum_{c} y_{f,u,k}^{c} \;+\; \sum_{c'} y_{g,u,k}^{c'} \;-\; 1 & & \forall\, u \in U_f \cap U_g \\
  \zeta_{f,g,u,k} \;&\in\; \{0, 1\} & & \forall\, u \in U_f \cap U_g
\end{align}
The upper bounds force $\zeta = 0$ if either side is 0; the lower bound forces
$\zeta = 1$ if both sides are 1. Standard McCormick AND linearization.

\paragraph{Both-arcs-used indicator $\xi_{f,g,k}$.}
Binary variable: $\xi = 1$ iff $k$ uses both $f$ and $g$ (regardless of ULD type).
McCormick linearization of $x_f^k \cdot x_g^k$:
\begin{align}
  \xi_{f,g,k} \;&\leq\; x_f^k \\
  \xi_{f,g,k} \;&\leq\; x_g^k \\
  \xi_{f,g,k} \;&\geq\; x_f^k + x_g^k - 1 \\
  \xi_{f,g,k} \;&\in\; \{0, 1\}
\end{align}
$\xi$ is a named intermediate used in the $\chi$ identity below and in the §9
objective prose; the variable could be inlined as $x_f^k \cdot x_g^k$ everywhere it
appears, but the named form keeps the bilinear-linearization layer visible.

\paragraph{Cross-ULD indicator $\chi_{f,g,k}$.}
Continuous variable in $[0, 1]$: $\chi = 1$ iff $k$ uses both arcs but a different
ULD type on each.
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{f,g,k} \;=\; \xi_{f,g,k} \;-\; \sum_{u \in U_f \cap U_g} \zeta_{f,g,u,k},
  \qquad \chi_{f,g,k} \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}
$\chi$ is \emph{integer-valued at any integer-feasible solution} because $\xi \in \{0,1\}$
and $\sum_u \zeta \in \{0, 1\}$ (P.9 forces a shipment to use at most one
$(u, c)$-combination per flight, so at most one $\zeta_{f,g,u,k}$ can equal 1).
At the LP relaxation, $\chi$ can be fractional --- this is acceptable because $\chi$
enters the objective with a positive coefficient (cross-ULD rehandling cost) and the
solver drives it to 0 unless integrality on $x$, $y$ forces it up. Declaring $\chi$
as continuous (not binary) avoids a misstatement of variable domain and a redundant
integrality requirement at the LP root.

\paragraph{Variable count per consecutive hub arc-pair per shipment.}
$|U_f \cap U_g|$ $\zeta$ variables, $1$ $\xi$ variable, $1$ $\chi$ variable;
$\approx 5|U|+5$ constraints. Reused by P.14 (same-ULD and cross-ULD MCT cases)
and by the objective's two rehandling-cost terms.

\subsection{Big-M Tightening (per-constraint values)}
\label{sec:bigm-tightening}

Loose big-M values weaken the LP relaxation and slow solving. The naïve horizon-wide
$M = T_k^{\text{dead}} - t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} + \epsilon$ is correct but typically
loose by $5\text{--}20\times$ relative to per-constraint tight values. Per Wolsey,
\emph{Integer Programming} (1998) \S1.7 and Bertsimas--Tsitsiklis \emph{LP Intro}
\S11, the tight $M$ for an indicator constraint is the maximum amount the constraint
must be relaxed when the indicator deactivates --- which is constraint-specific, not
shipment-horizon-wide.

\paragraph{Tight $M$ values used in the model.}
For each indicator constraint, use the following:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{P.11 (effective cutoff):}
        $M^{P11}_{f,k} \defeq \max\!\bigl(0,\; T_k^{\text{dead}} - \text{CO}^*_{f,k}\bigr) + \text{prep\_time}_k + \epsilon$.
        The constraint $t_k(i) + \text{prep\_time}_k \leq \text{CO}^*_{f,k} + M(1 - x)$
        needs $M$ large enough that, when $x = 0$, the LHS can reach its maximum
        $T_k^{\text{dead}} + \text{prep\_time}_k$ and the RHS still accommodates.
  \item \textbf{P.12 (ground arc propagation):}
        $M^{P12}_{ij,k} \defeq \tilde{\mu}^{\text{G}}_{ij,k} + \epsilon$.
        When $x_{ij}^k = 0$, the constraint $t_k(j) \geq t_k(i) + \tilde{\mu} - M$
        becomes $t_k(j) \geq t_k(i) - \epsilon$, vacuous given non-negativity. The
        tight $M$ equals the arc's transit time itself, not the horizon span.
  \item \textbf{P.13 (air arc propagation):}
        $M^{P13}_f \defeq \text{ETA}_f + \epsilon$.
        The constraint $t_k(j) \geq \text{ETA}_f - M(1 - x)$ becomes
        $t_k(j) \geq \text{ETA}_f - M$ when $x = 0$. Since
        $t_k(j) \geq 0$, $M = \text{ETA}_f$ is the tight bound.
        (Tighter still: $M = \text{ETA}_f - \tau_k(j)$ where $\tau_k(j)$ is the
        earliest arrival at $j$ from subgraph construction --- typically a
        smaller value.)
  \item \textbf{P.14a, P.14b (hub MCT):}
        $M^{P14}_{f,g} \defeq \text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} + \epsilon$ (the longer
        of the two MCTs is the tight bound for both cases). For P.14a, the
        deactivator $(2 - \sum_c y^c_{f,u,k} - \sum_{c'} y^{c'}_{g,u,k})$ can reach
        $2$, so the multiplier on $M$ is at most $2$; the tight $M$ is therefore
        $\text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} / 2 + \epsilon$ for that case if the
        deactivator pattern stays the two-indicator form. For P.14b the
        deactivator $(1 - \chi_{f,g,k}) \in [0, 1]$, so $M^{P14b} = \text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g} + \epsilon$
        is tight.
  \item \textbf{P.19 (locked commitments):}
        $M$ not applicable --- P.19 fixes variables to committed values
        (\S\ref{sec:locked-commitments}); no indicator-style $M$ needed (cf.\ T4
        on inequality vs.\ equality form).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{LP relaxation impact.}
Replacing horizon-wide $M$ with per-constraint tight $M$ typically reduces the LP
gap on time-binding instances by $30\text{--}50\%$ (per Trespalacios \& Grossmann
2014 on big-M tightening for indicator constraints). Combined with the disaggregation
options noted under T3 (deferred) and the cross-ULD $\chi$ formulation (T2), the
LP root is materially tighter without changing the integer optimum.

\paragraph{Implementation note.}
Each constraint subsection (P.11, P.12, P.13, P.14a, P.14b) now references its own
$M^{P\cdot}_{\cdot}$ symbol, not a generic $M$. The values are computed pre-solve
from shipment and flight parameters and pinned at MILP build time.

\subsection{Spot Rate Per-Shipment Pre-Computation}

The IATA weight-break rule (\S\ref{sec:spot-params}) is a $\min$ over tiers of piecewise
linear cost. Since shipment chargeable weight $cw_k$ is a parameter, $\text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f)$
is fully pre-computed before the MILP is built. No linearization needed at solve time.

\section{Re-ULDing — Operational Mechanics}
\label{sec:reulding}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

This section motivates and defines re-ULDing in operational detail, which is the basis for
the through-ULD compatibility parameter $\psi$ and the rehandling cost $\rho^{\text{reULD}}$.

\paragraph{What re-ULDing is.}
At an intermediate (hub) airport on a multi-hop route, a built ULD arriving on flight $f$
may need to be \emph{broken down} — i.e., physically dismantled, with cargo removed
piece-by-piece — and \emph{rebuilt} into a different ULD for the onward flight $g$. The
specific sequence:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item ULD arrives on inbound flight $f$ at the hub.
  \item Ground handler unloads ULD from aircraft into the hub cargo terminal.
  \item ULD is moved to the breakdown area.
  \item Cargo is removed from the ULD; each AWB's pieces are extracted and tagged.
  \item Cargo is held in the hub warehouse pending the onward flight $g$.
  \item For onward flight $g$: an empty ULD from carrier($g$)'s pool is delivered to build-up.
  \item Cargo is restuffed into the new ULD, secured with netting, weighed.
  \item Built ULD is moved to the aircraft loading area and loaded onto $g$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{When re-ULDing is required ($\psi = 0$).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Interline (different carriers):} carrier($f$) $\neq$ carrier($g$). Each
        airline maintains its own ULD pool; ULDs cannot transfer between pools. Cargo must
        leave airline $f$'s ULD and enter airline $g$'s ULD.
  \item \textbf{ULD type change:} if the inbound aircraft uses LD3s (wide-body belly) and
        the outbound aircraft uses PMC main-deck pallets (freighter), the ULD type itself
        must change. Cargo is re-stuffed into the new ULD type.
  \item \textbf{Hub lacks through-cargo facility for type $u$:} rare at major hubs (HKG,
        FRA, NRT all have through-cargo handling) but possible at smaller airports.
  \item \textbf{Forwarder elects re-ULD:} e.g., to re-sort cargo destined to different
        onward flights into different ULDs. Not modeled in MVP (deferred).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{When through-ULD applies ($\psi = 1$).}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Through-flight technical stop:} same flight number, same aircraft. The ULD
        stays in its aircraft position throughout the intermediate stop. The hub processing
        is limited to refueling and crew change. Example: 5Y9123 TPE$\to$ANC$\to$JFK, ULD
        loaded at TPE stays on the aircraft through ANC and continues to JFK.
  \item \textbf{Same-airline through-cargo at hub:} carrier($f$) = carrier($g$), the airline
        has a through-cargo agreement at the hub, and the ULD type is compatible with both
        aircraft. ULD is unloaded from $f$, moved as a complete unit through the hub
        through-cargo facility, and loaded onto $g$. Common at major hubs for same-airline
        connections (e.g., Cathay TPE$\to$HKG$\to$JFK on CX564 + CX880).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cost and time impact.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Through-ULD MCT: $\text{MCT}^{\text{thru}}$ typically $1.5$--$4$ hours depending on
        hub and aircraft turnaround.
  \item Re-ULD MCT: $\text{MCT}^{\text{reULD}}$ typically $6$--$12$ hours including
        breakdown, hub storage, and rebuild for onward flight.
  \item Rehandling cost: $\rho^{\text{reULD}}$ typically \$150--\$500 per ULD, depending on
        cargo characteristics and hub labor rates.
  \item Cargo risk: re-ULDing introduces handling-induced damage and piece-loss risk;
        valuable / fragile cargo prefers through-ULD where possible (modeled implicitly via
        the cost differential).
\end{itemize}

\section{Open Items and Future Extensions}
\label{sec:deferred}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Period-level commitment optimization (P.7).} MVP takes $\underline{M}_{c,u,t}$
        from a separate upstream model. Future: joint optimization of period commitment
        allocation across the rolling horizon.
  \item \label{item:psi-decision-var} \textbf{Through-ULD as decision variable.} MVP makes $\psi$ a pre-computed
        parameter. P1: promote to decision variable, allowing forwarder-elected re-ULDing
        at compatible hubs for cargo re-sorting.
  \item \label{item:lock-buyout} \textbf{Contract buyout / lock breaking.} MVP enforces
        hard locks: arcs in $A_k^{\text{loc}}$ are irrevocable within a solve. P1: allow
        penalty-paid lock breaks (forwarder pays a cancellation fee to free a locked arc),
        modeled as a per-arc buyout cost parameter $\kappa_{k,(i,j)}^{\text{break}}$ and a
        binary break-decision $\beta_{k,(i,j)} \in \{0,1\}$ that releases the lock when
        $\beta = 1$ at cost $\kappa^{\text{break}}$. Use case: ocean-to-air recovery when a
        committed ocean booking will miss the deadline and re-booking via air is cheaper
        than the contract penalty.
  \item \label{item:sla-soft-otp} \textbf{Soft SLA with OTP penalty.} MVP enforces
        $T_p^{\text{SLA}}$ hard (P.20); breach $=$ rescue event. P1: introduce slack
        $\sigma_k \geq 0$ on the SLA constraint and an OTP penalty $\pi^{\text{OTP}}_p$
        per hour of breach. Objective gains $\sum_k \pi^{\text{OTP}}_{\text{sp}(k)} \cdot \sigma_k$;
        revised SLA constraint becomes $t_k(d(k)) - t_k^{\text{rdy,early}} - \sigma_k \leq T_p^{\text{SLA}}$.
        Lets the MILP elect a paid SLA miss when premium-upgrade cost exceeds the OTP
        penalty. Calibrate $\pi^{\text{OTP}}$ from real shipper-contract penalty schedules
        (typically \$50--\$500 per shipment per day of late delivery, tiered).
  \item \textbf{Probabilistic transit time objective.} MVP uses deterministic mean transit
        and a hard deadline. P1: extend to $\Pr(\text{arrival} \leq T_k^{\text{dead}}) \geq p$.
  \item \label{item:probabilistic-clearance} \textbf{Probabilistic clearance dwell with
        exam-hold probability.} MVP uses a deterministic mean
        $\delta^{\text{cust}}_k$ (\S\ref{sec:ground-arc-params}). P1: model exam-hold as a
        Bernoulli event with destination-country-specific probability and conditional
        dwell distribution (typical: 95--97\% release within hours for pre-filed entries;
        3--5\% held with 1--3 day distribution). Integrates with the probabilistic transit
        time objective above.
  \item \textbf{Full 3D bin-packing within ULD.} MVP uses aggregate volume constraint (P.2)
        with $\eta = 0.97$ as a fill-rate proxy. P1: 3D bin-packing model for shipments
        with awkward piece dimensions.
  \item \textbf{ULD availability at CFS.} MVP assumes empty ULDs are always delivered to CFS
        before cargo cutoff. P1: model ULD pool availability as a constraint.
  \item \textbf{CFS dock door capacity.} MVP assumes infinite CFS throughput. P1: model dock
        door and labor capacity as a CFS-level constraint.
  \item \textbf{Cargo type segregation within ULD.} MVP allows any compatible commodities
        to share a ULD. P1: hazmat compatibility matrix, perishable temperature segregation.
  \item \textbf{Multiple ULD types per shipment.} MVP forces a shipment to one ULD type per
        flight. P1: allow splitting across LD3 + LD7 if economically preferred.
  \item \textbf{Carrier alliance slot sharing.} MVP treats each contract independently.
        P1: model SkyTeam / Star Alliance / oneworld slot exchanges where ULD positions on
        partner carriers can substitute.
  \item \textbf{Spot capacity availability uncertainty.} MVP treats spot rate as always
        available. Real spot capacity has day-of-departure availability risk. P1: model
        spot capacity as a stochastic constraint or with explicit booking rejection events.
  \item \label{item:partial-split-shipment} \textbf{Partial / split shipment across flights
        (do not re-flag in future critique passes).} The MVP formulation uses a single-path
        $x_{ij}^k$ binary with flow conservation, so a HAWB cannot be split across two
        flights. Real ops occasionally splits a large shipment when no single flight can
        accept the full volume, or when partial-roll occurs. The operational workaround
        is to split the HAWB into virtual sub-HAWBs at intake (each sub-HAWB routes
        independently); the MILP is unchanged. P1: introduce native split assignment with
        a parent-HAWB binding for billing. \textbf{Note for future critique agents:} the
        absence of split-shipment support in the MILP is an intentional MVP scope decision,
        not a gap.
  \item \label{item:cfs-storage-demurrage} \textbf{CFS storage / demurrage cost in the
        objective.} MVP omits CFS dwell-cost in the objective; the optimizer may prefer
        a cheaper later flight without seeing the offsetting storage cost at origin or
        destination CFS. P1: add per-CFS storage rate and free-day parameters and a
        dwell-cost term in the objective derived from
        $\max\bigl(0,\, t_k(j_{\text{depart-cfs}}) - t_k(j_{\text{arrive-cfs}}) - \text{cfs\_free\_days}\bigr) \cdot \text{cfs\_storage\_rate} \cdot v_k$.
        Sourced rate notes (research 2026-05-17): typical CFS storage rates run
        \$12/CBM/day with \$70/day minimum at major US facilities (Imperial CFS,
        \url{https://www.imperialcfs.com/IPI/Warehouse}); demurrage post-free-period
        \$5--15/CBM/day depending on location; free periods 3--5 days export, 5--7
        days import (FreightAmigo summary,
        \url{https://www.freightamigo.com/en/blog/logistics/understanding-storage-fees-in-logistics-what-you-need-to-know/}).
        For a 5 CBM shipment dwelling 5 days past free at \$12/CBM/day = \$300 ---
        material on a 50-shipment/day book where $\sim$10\% land in storage. The
        parameter and constraint are sketched out; implementation is deferred to P1.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Tractability and Scaling Roadmap}
\label{sec:scaling-roadmap}
%%--------------------------------------------------------------------

This section records the tractability levers, decomposition options, and
production-deployment strategies surfaced by the formulation review (\S\ref{sec:deferred}
context). None are active model changes; all are P1-or-later, gated on empirical
solve-time data from production. The intent is to keep the analysis with the model so
future-you doesn't re-derive it. Citations are inline where the lever maps to an
established OR result.

\paragraph{Base instance scale estimate (MVP, \(|K|=100\) shipments).}
With aggressive pre-filtering ($|F^k| \approx 30$ post-filter, $|A^k| \approx 36$
total arcs per shipment, $\sim$8 hub-pairs per shipment, $|U_f| \approx 3$,
$|C_f| \approx 2$): $\sim$30K binaries / integers, $\sim$3K continuous,
$\sim$45--50K constraints (McCormick auxiliaries dominate the row count). HiGHS-comfortable on
a 16 GB M1 laptop. Expected solve time at 1\% MIP gap: \textbf{15--60s} for $|K|=100$;
\textbf{1--5 min} for $|K|=300$; \textbf{5--15 min} for $|K|=500$ (without simplifications
below). Beyond $|K| \approx 750$ single-tenant on laptop hardware, simplifications and
decomposition become required. Numbers cross-checked against Mittelmann MILP benchmarks
(\url{https://plato.asu.edu/ftp/milp.html}, updated 2024--2025) and HiGHS performance
papers (Huangfu \& Hall 2018).

\subsection*{Simplification levers (P1, ordered by impact)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{item:scale-y-aggregation} \textbf{Aggregate $y_{f,u,k}^c$ over contracts.}
        Decompose $y_{f,u,k}^c = y_{f,u,k} \cdot \alpha_{f,u}^c$ where $\alpha_{f,u}^c \in \{0,1\}$
        picks the contract per (flight, ULD type). Variable count drops from
        $K \cdot |F^k| \cdot |U_f| \cdot |C_f|$ to $K \cdot |F^k| \cdot |U_f| + |F| \cdot |U_f| \cdot |C_f|$.
        Estimated $\sim$40--48\% binary reduction at $K=100$--$500$ (per the formulation
        review). Operationally faithful: one MAWB $\to$ one contract per (flight, ULD).
        Source: standard fixed-charge MCNF decomposition (Ahuja--Magnanti--Orlin \emph{Network Flows}
        \S17 on multi-commodity flow assignment); analogous to facility-location reformulation.

  \item \label{item:scale-shipment-aggregation} \textbf{Shipment aggregation into commodity classes.}
        Merge shipments with identical (origin, destination, service product, $\ctype_k$,
        $\text{screening\_status}_k$, $\text{lithium\_pi}_k$, ready-date-bucket) and similar
        $cw_k$ into representative classes with multiplicative weight; solve on classes;
        disaggregate to individual shipments in post-processing. For a typical tier-2
        forwarder day with $\sim$50--100 shipments concentrated on 20--30 lanes $\times$
        5--8 service products $\times$ 3--5 ready buckets: realistic class count
        $\sim$25--35 from $|K|=100$, giving a $\sim$3--4$\times$ effective scale
        compression. Source: passenger air OD-aggregation (Belobaba); applied to freight
        in Powell's dynamic vehicle allocation work and Caplice MIT FreightLab on lane-class
        aggregation.

  \item \label{item:scale-component-decomposition} \textbf{Component-wise solve on the supply graph.}
        After subgraph construction, build the supply graph $H$ where shipments are
        connected if they share any flight, ULD position, or capacity constraint; solve
        each connected component as an independent MILP. Expected $\sim$3--10 components
        per tenant batch (TPE-US-East separates from SZX-EU, etc.). Combined with shipment
        aggregation, $K=500$ split into 5 components of $\sim$100 each, each solved at
        $\sim$30s under simplifications: $\sim$30s total parallel, $\sim$150s serial.
        Source: standard MCNF decomposition (Ahuja--Magnanti--Orlin \S17); pattern
        inherited from the Ocean FCL model in this project.

  \item \label{item:scale-warm-start} \textbf{Warm-start re-solves from prior solution.}
        Rolling-horizon re-solves overlap heavily with previous solves (only locked
        prefixes and new shipments change). HiGHS supports MIP warm-start via
        \texttt{Highs::setSolution}. Previous solve's $x, y, z$ for shipments still in
        the planning window provide a near-optimal warm start; heuristic seed
        (single-shipment cheapest-path ignoring shared capacity) for new shipments.
        Expected $3$--$10\times$ speedup on re-solves vs.\ cold MIP. Source: HiGHS
        documentation; empirical results in Cplex/Gurobi parallel literature on
        rolling-horizon problems (Erera et al.\ 2017 service network design).

  \item \label{item:scale-spot-binary-fixing} \textbf{Variable fixing on dominated $b_{f,k}$ spot binaries.}
        Pre-compute $\text{cost}^{\text{spot}}(k, f)$ vs.\ contract cost $r_c \cdot cw_k$
        per $(k, f, c)$. Where every contract option is strictly cheaper than spot, fix
        $b_{f,k} = 0$ pre-solve. For BSA-heavy mid-market forwarders, $\sim$70--80\% of
        $(k, f)$ pairs have a dominant contract option, eliminating $\sim$70\% of spot
        binaries at $K=100$. Source: standard variable fixing via reduced-cost dominance.

  \item \label{item:scale-drop-chi} \textbf{Drop $\chi$ cross-ULD distinction (fidelity vs.\ speed tradeoff).}
        Same-ULD vs.\ cross-ULD rehandling costs differ by $\sim$10--20\% on the
        $\sim$5\% of routings that are cross-type. Merging
        $\rho^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g,u}$ and $\bar{\rho}^{\text{reULD}}_{f,g}$ into a single
        $\bar{\rho}$ eliminates $\xi$ and $\chi$ and one rehandling-cost objective term.
        Estimated $\sim$800 binaries and $\sim$4K rows out at $K=100$. Cost objective
        error: $<0.5\%$ on typical instances. Highest yield-per-fidelity-loss
        simplification.

  \item \label{item:scale-p17-pre-elim} \textbf{Confirm P.17 (ULD physical fit) is pure pre-elimination.}
        P.17 declares ``this is a pre-processing exclusion, not a runtime constraint.''
        Verify the implementation removes the $y_{f,u,k}^c = 0$ rows entirely rather
        than emitting them as redundant constraints to the solver. Potentially $\sim$12K
        rows eliminated at $K=100$ if half of $(k, u)$ pairs are infeasible by piece
        dimension. Implementation check, not a model change.

  \item \label{item:scale-mip-gap} \textbf{Production MIP gap $\geq 1\%$, never solve to 0\%.}
        Freight routing literature (Powell ADP corpus, Topaloglu, Erera) consistently
        treats $1$--$5\%$ gap as production-acceptable: cost variance from sell-rate
        negotiation, FX, fuel surcharge volatility, and override behavior dwarfs the
        optimization gap. Recommended: $1\%$ for premium / SLA-critical batches; $3\%$
        for standard / multimodal-economy batches. Expected speedup vs.\ exact: $3$--$5\times$
        on $K=300$, $\sim$$5\times$ on $K=500$. Coordinate with
        $\varepsilon^{\text{pref}}$ (\S\ref{sec:carrier-policy}) which must be $\geq$
        the Pass-1 gap (\S T5 in this section's review history).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Strategy notes (production deployment)}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \label{item:strat-prefilter-instrumentation} \textbf{Pre-filter aggressiveness has 100$\times$ leverage; instrument empirically.}
        The flight reachability pass applies $\sim$9 predicates (cutoff, deadline, SLA,
        embargo, lithium, screening, service-product mode/carrier/ac\_type, cargo type,
        physical fit). Each typically drops 30--60\% of flights; compounded survival
        rate $\sim$1--5\% post-filter. Pre-filter quality has more leverage than any
        single solver-tuning change. Required instrumentation: log $|A^k|$ post-filter
        per shipment for the first $\sim$100 production solves. If survival rate
        $>5\%$, fix the filter before any other scale work.

  \item \label{item:strat-column-generation} \textbf{Column generation deferred until empirical $>15$ min at production scale.}
        Dantzig-Wolfe / column generation (pricing subproblem $=$ shortest-path-with-resource-constraints
        per shipment on $G(N_k, A_k)$ with capacity duals from master) is the classical
        heavy weapon for large MCNF. Premature at MVP scale; trigger only when monolithic
        MILP exceeds 15 min on production-typical instances. Source: Desrosiers \& Lübbecke
        (2005) primer; Powell on dynamic vehicle allocation as the analogous use case.

  \item \label{item:strat-commercial-solver} \textbf{Commercial solver (Gurobi / CPLEX / COPT) transition triggers.}
        HiGHS handles MVP scale at 1\% gap. Commercial solvers become worth evaluating
        when (a) per-tenant $|K| > 500$ sustained, (b) re-solve latency budget $<30$s for
        urgent rescue shipments, or (c) LP relaxation gap on time-binding instances
        consistently $>10\%$ (Gurobi cuts + branching typically $2$--$5\times$ faster
        than HiGHS on big-M MCNF per Mittelmann benchmarks). Business case: Gurobi
        licensing $\sim\$10$K/year/seat justifiable above $\sim\$1$M ARR / 5+ active
        tenants. Until then, HiGHS plus the simplifications above.

  \item \label{item:strat-v2-mawb-rescale} \textbf{Re-do scale analysis after v2 MAWB restructure.}
        The MAWB-level restructure (\S\ref{sec:mawb-hawb}, P1 deferred decision-variables
        rewrite) introduces $h_{k,m}$ HAWB-to-MAWB binaries and re-roots $y$ around $m$
        instead of $k$. At $|K|=100, |M|\approx 40$, $h$ alone adds $\sim$4K binaries;
        at $|K|=500, |M|\approx 150$, $h$ adds $\sim$75K binaries. Net effect:
        $\sim$15--20\% variable-count growth at MVP scale, more at higher $K$. The
        density-mixing benefit is real (10--30\% cost savings on consolidatable
        traffic), but the scale numbers above all need recomputing after that change
        lands.

  \item \label{item:strat-sla-pickup-anchor} \textbf{SLA pickup-time anchor --- operational tradeoff, not a bug.}
        P.20 (Transit-Time SLA) anchors against the parameter $t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$,
        not the decision variable $t_k(o(k))$. Operationally defensible: shipper
        SLAs are measured from when cargo is offered for pickup, not from when the
        forwarder elects to dispatch. Side effect: the optimizer is not penalized for
        deferring pickup to $t_k^{\text{rdy,late}}$ if that buys cheaper downstream
        slots. Acceptable unless tenant policy requires earliest-pickup discipline; in
        that case, replace $t_k^{\text{rdy,early}}$ with $t_k(o(k))$ in
        Eq.~\ref{eq:P20} (still linear).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Punch list summary.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Ship as-is} if instance size $\leq 100$ shipments / tenant / solve.
  \item For $100$--$500$ shipments per tenant: implement items~\ref{item:scale-y-aggregation},
        \ref{item:scale-shipment-aggregation}, \ref{item:scale-component-decomposition},
        \ref{item:scale-warm-start}, \ref{item:scale-spot-binary-fixing},
        \ref{item:scale-drop-chi}, \ref{item:scale-mip-gap}.
  \item Beyond $\sim$750 shipments per tenant: column generation
        (item~\ref{item:strat-column-generation}) or commercial solver
        (item~\ref{item:strat-commercial-solver}).
  \item Pre-filter instrumentation (item~\ref{item:strat-prefilter-instrumentation}) is
        the first thing to wire up regardless of scale.
\end{itemize}
